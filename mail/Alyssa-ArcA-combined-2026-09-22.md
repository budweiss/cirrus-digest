LESSON 1 — Opening Bookend (pp. 14-19)

1. PA Core Standards

None assigned. Great Minds' PA alignment guide does not code Lesson 1 to any standard. It is an activation/hook lesson (a 2-minute launch plus a teacher's-choice engagement activity), not a content-bearing lesson.

2. Learning Goals / I can statements

- I can share what I already know or have experienced about having a great heart.
- I can explore the topic of our new module.

Why it matters: This lesson surfaces prior knowledge and personal connection before any text work starts, so students have a stake in the Essential Question ("What does having a great heart mean?") before they are asked to analyze anything.

3. Student considerations

No text is assigned, so there is no decoding or fluency demand yet. The access requirement is purely oral: can each student talk about "a great heart" using their own vocabulary and experience? A student with limited expressive vocabulary, or who is newer to English, may need sentence starters or a visual prompt to participate, not because the content is hard but because the format (open discussion) assumes verbal ease. No prior background knowledge is required, since the lesson is built to surface whatever a student already has, but it is worth knowing ahead of time which students have a personal or community connection to caregiving, medicine, or "heart" in a literal sense. That is an asset here, not a gap to fill.

4. Learning Progress / Instructional Plan (pp. 14-15)

Launch (2 min): choral-read the Essential Question. Learn: Share (discuss prior knowledge about a great heart), then Engage (the teacher's chosen activity: drawing, experiencing a beating heart, reading a text about someone with a great heart, or a teacher-created experience). Land: revisit the Essential Question. There is no assigned text, no annotation, and no formal facilitation strategy beyond the chosen activity. The rigor comes later; this lesson's job is buy-in.

5. Success Criteria / Evidence of learning

Informal only; the Teach Book gives no rubric or task here. Evidence is participation quality: are students offering real experiences and ideas about "a great heart," or just complying? Worth a quick anecdotal note on who has background knowledge to draw on later and who does not.

6. Next steps

This lesson plants the Essential Question that every one of the next 39 lessons answers a piece of. Arc B pivots to the literal, biological heart, and the Module Finale asks students to bring both threads (figurative and literal) back together. Whatever a student says today is the baseline the whole module keeps returning to, so it is worth a quick note on who is starting from personal experience versus starting cold.

LESSON 2 — Wonder (pp. 20-31)

1. PA Core Standards

- CC.1.1.4.D — Phonics and word analysis
- CC.1.1.4.E — Fluency (accuracy)
- CC.1.3.4.K — Reads and comprehends grade-level literary fiction

The Notice-and-Wonder work falls under the general reading-range standard (K); the guide does not tie it to a separate literary-analysis code.

2. Learning Goals / I can statements

- I can read "Heart to Heart" with accuracy, decoding each word correctly.
- I can notice and wonder about a work of art, writing at least two things I notice about Mother and Child and two related questions.

Why it matters: This is the module's first real encounter with both anchor texts. Accuracy-first fluency and open noticing/wondering are deliberately low-stakes; the goal is curiosity and a clean first read, not analysis yet.

3. Student considerations

This is the first read-aloud check, and it tests accuracy only, not fluency, which is not assessed until Lesson 3 onward. A teacher needs to know which students struggle specifically with decoding accuracy (versus rate or expression), since that is the diagnostic signal for how to scaffold Lessons 3-6. Background knowledge needed: prior-grade vocabulary for talking about poems (stanza, line, speaker). The Teach Book flags this explicitly as a moment to observe who already has it. Five vocabulary words (regret, yearn, clutch, mute, tattoo) need front-loading for students with smaller expressive vocabularies, ideally before the annotation task rather than during it.

4. Learning Progress / Instructional Plan (pp. 20-24)

Launch (4 min): orient to the Essential Question and the year's routine. Learn: Read (13 min: silent read and annotate unknown words, modeled teacher think-aloud, Notice and Wonder Chart, partner share), then Observe (the same Notice and Wonder routine applied to the sculpture). Land: reflect. Facilitation leans on modeling (the teacher think-aloud is printed verbatim in the Teach Book) before releasing to partners, a gradual-release structure rather than a straight lecture.

5. Success Criteria / Evidence of learning

Two genuine notices and two related questions on the Notice and Wonder Chart, for both the poem and the sculpture. Accuracy in the read-aloud (not fluency yet; that is the job of Lessons 3-6).

6. Next steps

Today's accuracy data is the first point in a six-lesson fluency arc (accuracy, phrasing, expression, rate, performance, ending in Lesson 6's small-group read-aloud). How a student does here should shape how much support they get in each later read-aloud, not just today's. The Notice and Wonder questions students raise here are the ones their own analysis work answers across Lessons 3-5.

LESSON 3 — Organize (pp. 32-45)

1. PA Core Standards

- CC.1.1.4.D — Phonics and word analysis
- CC.1.1.4.E — Fluency (phrasing)
- CC.1.3.4.B — Cites details and makes inferences
- CC.1.3.4.E — Structural elements of poems and prose (the Poem Organizer work)
- CC.1.3.4.I — Vocabulary strategies
- CC.1.3.4.K — Reads and comprehends grade-level literary fiction

2. Learning Goals / I can statements

- I can read "Heart to Heart" with appropriate phrasing, grouping words into phrases and pausing for punctuation.
- I can describe the forms within Mother and Child.

Why it matters: Phrasing is the fluency skill that turns decoding into meaning-making. A student who reads word by word cannot yet organize a poem's ideas, and this lesson makes that link explicit before asking for any interpretation.

3. Student considerations

Students need enough decoding fluency to shift attention from individual words to phrase-level meaning (the phrasing task). A student still building basic accuracy from Lesson 2 will need the built-in Prologue support lesson before this one. Three new vocabulary words (abstract art, form, heart as a noun) need to be solid before Lesson 4, where those same words carry the central figurative-language concept. A student without them will hit Lesson 4 unprepared, not just behind.

4. Learning Progress / Instructional Plan (pp. 32-33)

Read: model and practice appropriate phrasing in "Heart to Heart." Respond: organize the poem's elements using a class Poem Organizer (stanza by stanza, "what is happening" style; a useful template to reuse for other poems). Observe: describe the sculpture's forms. Land: view the "Poet Laureate" video, in which Rita Dove herself talks about her craft, an authentic hook tying the poet to the poem.

5. Success Criteria / Evidence of learning

A completed Poem Organizer showing literal, stanza-level understanding; correctly phrased oral reading; a written description of the sculpture's forms.

6. Next steps

The Poem Organizer built today (literal, stanza-by-stanza content) is the literal baseline Lesson 4 needs before it can ask students to find a figurative meaning in the same stanzas. This lesson's real job is setting up Lesson 4's contrast, not just organizing today's poem.

LESSON 4 — Reveal (pp. 46-57)

1. PA Core Standards

- CC.1.1.4.D — Phonics and word analysis
- CC.1.1.4.E — Fluency (expression)
- CC.1.3.4.B — Cites details and makes inferences
- CC.1.3.4.E — Structural elements of poems and prose
- CC.1.3.4.F — Figurative language
- CC.1.3.4.I — Vocabulary strategies
- CC.1.3.4.K — Reads and comprehends grade-level literary fiction

Lesson 4 is the first lesson in the whole module that the guide codes under CC.1.3.4.F, which matches its literal/figurative "heart" focus exactly.

2. Learning Goals / I can statements

- I can read "Heart to Heart" with expression, using my voice to reflect feeling.
- I can explain the effects of negative space in Mother and Child.

Why it matters: This is the pivot lesson for the module's Essential Question. The vocabulary introduced here (figurative, literal, idiom, negative space) is the conceptual core of "A Great Heart," and it is taught side by side across the poem and the sculpture: the same literal/figurative lens applied to two different media.

3. Student considerations

This lesson directly teaches literal versus figurative meaning. Students who still default to literal reading (developmentally common at this age, independent of language background) will need the built-in Prologue support. Students need Lesson 3's vocabulary (abstract art, form) already in place to access the sculpture analysis; a student missing that vocabulary needs it retaught before this lesson, not alongside it. The materials include a Syllable Types reference chart and a side-by-side display of "literal" and "figurative" with syllable divisions, a useful decoding scaffold for students still building multisyllabic word attack.

4. Learning Progress / Instructional Plan (pp. 46-47)

Read: expressive read-aloud of "Heart to Heart." Respond: examine the poem's literal versus figurative language using an Idiom Chart. Observe: examine negative space in the sculpture and its effect on the viewer's interpretation. The parallel structure (same concept, two media) is the strategy worth naming if an evaluator asks how the lesson promotes rigor: it is transfer, not repetition.

5. Success Criteria / Evidence of learning

A correctly completed Idiom Chart distinguishing literal and figurative references to "heart"; a written explanation of the effect of negative space that uses the term correctly.

6. Next steps

The literal/figurative distinction taught here is exactly what Lesson 5 assumes is solid when it asks students to state a central idea. A claim about what "a great heart" means does not work if a student does not yet know which sense of "heart" is meant. This is the hinge lesson for the whole arc's Essential Question.

LESSON 5 — Distill (pp. 58-69)

1. PA Core Standards

- CC.1.1.4.E — Fluency (rate)
- CC.1.3.4.A — Determines a theme from details in the text
- CC.1.3.4.B — Cites details and makes inferences
- CC.1.3.4.I — Vocabulary strategies
- CC.1.3.4.K — Reads and comprehends grade-level literary fiction

Lesson 5 is the first lesson in the module that the guide codes under CC.1.3.4.A, matching its central-idea work. (The phonics standard, CC.1.1.4.D, does not apply to this lesson.)

2. Learning Goals / I can statements

- I can read "Heart to Heart" fluently at an appropriate rate.
- I can, during a class discussion, explain what Mother and Child conveys about the relationship between a mother and child.

Why it matters: This is the first lesson that asks students to make an interpretive claim (a central idea) rather than describe or organize, the biggest cognitive jump in the arc so far.

3. Student considerations

There is no new vocabulary and no decoding demand. The access barrier here is oral argumentation, not print. A student can read fluently and still struggle if they have not been taught how to state and defend a claim in discussion, and the reverse is also true. It is worth knowing ahead of time which students are strong readers but hesitant discussants (they need discussion scaffolds) versus the opposite (they need help grounding a claim in the actual text).

4. Learning Progress / Instructional Plan (pp. 58-59)

Read: fluent practice read. Respond: class discussion distilling a central idea from "Heart to Heart." Observe: class discussion on what the sculpture communicates about motherhood. Both are discussion-based rather than written, an oral rehearsal for the written synthesis in Lesson 6.

5. Success Criteria / Evidence of learning

Participation in a substantive discussion that names and defends a central idea with reference to the text and the artwork. This is informal, discussion-based evidence rather than a worksheet.

6. Next steps

The central idea a student states out loud today is exactly what Lesson 6 asks them to put into writing; Lesson 5 is oral rehearsal for Lesson 6's written synthesis. A student who could not articulate a central idea here needs support before Lesson 6, not during it.

LESSON 6 — Know (pp. 70-77)

1. PA Core Standards

- CC.1.1.4.E — Fluency (performance reading)
- CC.1.3.4.I — Vocabulary strategies
- CC.1.3.4.K — Reads and comprehends grade-level literary fiction
- CC.1.4.4.F — Conventions of standard English (the complete-sentences writing task)

The guide codes this lesson's writing under conventions (F), not the informative-writing content standards, which it does not apply until Lesson 11. Lesson 6 is a conventions checkpoint, not yet a full informative-writing lesson.

2. Learning Goals / I can statements

- I can perform a fluent reading of "Heart to Heart" for a small group.
- I can write complete sentences to express knowledge gained from studying Mother and Child.

Why it matters: This closes the Wonder, Organize, Reveal, Distill, Know cycle. Students go from first noticing the text (Lesson 2) to performing it and writing their own synthesis (Lesson 6), which is the module's instructional model in miniature and a good example to point to when explaining the curriculum's design.

3. Student considerations

Students need enough fluency to perform for a small group, not just read silently; a student still building basic accuracy from Lesson 2 needs real support here, since this is a public performance rather than private reading. Students also need enough command of sentence-level mechanics (subject/verb agreement, end punctuation) to write complete sentences. A strong reader who struggles with writing mechanics will need conventions support that this task alone will not provide. Pre-plan the small groups rather than leaving them random, since reading aloud is a real stretch for some students.

4. Learning Progress / Instructional Plan (pp. 70-71)

Preparation: post charts on literal/figurative language, hearts, Rita Dove, and poets laureate around the room ahead of time. Read: small-group fluent performance. Respond/Observe: write complete sentences expressing knowledge gained from each text. Land: synthesize learning, the arc's closing move.

5. Success Criteria / Evidence of learning

A fluent small-group performance; complete written sentences that reflect genuine knowledge gained (not just restating the text) about both the poem and the sculpture.

6. Next steps

The complete-sentence habit built here is a floor, not a ceiling. Arc B and beyond assume it and build toward paragraph-level informative writing. This lesson also closes the figurative "heart" thread; Arc B opens with the literal, biological heart, so today's synthesis is what makes that pivot land as a continuation rather than a topic change.

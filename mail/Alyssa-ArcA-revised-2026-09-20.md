## Lesson 1 — Opening Bookend (pp. 14-19)

**Student considerations (revised):** No text is assigned, so there's no
decoding or fluency demand yet — the access requirement here is purely
oral: can each student talk about "a great heart" using their own
vocabulary and experience? A student with limited expressive vocabulary or
who is newer to English may need sentence starters or a visual prompt to
participate, not because the content is hard but because the format
(open discussion) assumes verbal ease. No prior background knowledge is
required — the lesson is built to surface whatever a student already has —
but it's worth knowing ahead of time which students have a personal or
community connection to caregiving, medicine, or "heart" in a literal
sense, since that's an asset here, not a gap to fill.

**Next steps (revised):** This lesson plants the Essential Question ("What
does having a great heart mean?") that every one of the next 39 lessons
answers a piece of — Arc B pivots to the literal, biological heart, and the
Module Finale asks students to bring both threads (figurative and literal)
back together. Whatever a student says today is the baseline the whole
module keeps returning to, so it's worth a quick note on who's starting
from personal experience versus starting cold.

## Lesson 2 — Wonder (pp. 20-31)

**Student considerations (revised):** This is the first read-aloud check,
and it tests accuracy only — not fluency, which doesn't get assessed until
L3 onward. A teacher needs to know which students struggle specifically
with decoding accuracy (versus rate or expression) since that's the
diagnostic signal for how to scaffold L3-L6. Background knowledge needed:
prior-grade vocabulary for talking about poems (stanza, line, speaker) —
the Teach Book flags this explicitly as a moment to observe who already has
it. Five vocabulary words (regret, yearn, clutch, mute, tattoo) need
front-loading for students with smaller expressive vocabularies — better
before the annotation task than during it.

**Next steps (revised):** The accuracy data from today is the first point
in a six-lesson fluency arc (accuracy → phrasing → expression → rate →
performance, ending in L6's small-group read-aloud) — how a student does
here should shape how much support they get in each later read-aloud, not
just today's. The Notice/Wonder questions students raise here are the ones
their own analysis work answers across L3-L5.

## Lesson 3 — Organize (pp. 32-45)

**Student considerations (revised):** Requires enough decoding fluency to
shift attention from individual words to phrase-level meaning (the phrasing
task) — a student still building basic accuracy from L2 will need the
built-in Prologue support lesson before this one. Three new vocabulary
words (abstract art, form, heart as a noun) need to be solid before L4,
where those same words carry the lesson's central figurative-language
concept — a student without them will hit L4 unprepared, not just behind.

**Next steps (revised):** The Poem Organizer built today (literal,
stanza-by-stanza content) is the literal baseline L4 needs before it can
ask students to find a *figurative* meaning in the same stanzas — this
lesson's real job is setting up L4's contrast, not just organizing today's
poem.

## Lesson 4 — Reveal (pp. 46-57)

**Student considerations (revised):** This is the lesson that directly
teaches literal-vs-figurative meaning — students who still default to
literal reading (developmentally common at this age, independent of
language background) will need the built-in Prologue support. Requires
L3's vocabulary (abstract art, form) already in place to access the
sculpture-side analysis; a student missing that vocabulary needs it
retaught before this lesson, not alongside it.

**Next steps (revised):** The literal/figurative distinction taught here is
exactly what L5 assumes is already solid when it asks students to state a
central idea — a claim about what "great heart" means doesn't work if a
student doesn't yet know which sense of "heart" is meant. This is the hinge
lesson for the whole arc's Essential Question.

## Lesson 5 — Distill (pp. 58-69)

**Student considerations (revised):** No new vocabulary and no
reading-decoding demand — the access barrier here is entirely oral
argumentation, not print. A student can read fluently and still struggle
if they haven't been taught how to state and defend a claim in discussion,
and the reverse is also true. Worth knowing ahead of time which students
are strong readers but hesitant discussants (need discussion scaffolds)
versus the opposite (need help grounding a claim in the actual text).

**Next steps (revised):** The central idea a student states out loud today
is exactly what L6 asks them to put into writing — L5 is oral rehearsal for
L6's written synthesis. A student who couldn't articulate a central idea
here needs support *before* L6, not during it.

## Lesson 6 — Know (pp. 70-77)

**Student considerations (revised):** Requires enough fluency to perform
for a small group, not just read silently — a student still building basic
accuracy from L2 needs real support here, since this is public performance,
not private reading. Requires enough command of sentence-level mechanics
(subject/verb, end punctuation) to write complete sentences — a strong
reader who struggles with writing mechanics will need conventions support
this task alone won't provide.

**Next steps (revised):** The complete-sentence habit built here is a
floor, not a ceiling — Arc B and beyond assume it and build toward
paragraph-level informative writing (the standards Arc B actually targets).
This lesson also closes the figurative "heart" thread; Arc B opens with the
literal, biological heart, so today's synthesis is what makes that pivot
land as a continuation rather than a topic change.

Here's Arc B in full — "The Circulatory Story" (10 lessons, pp. 78-185 in
your Teach Book). Both sections you asked to reframe are built in from the
start this time: Student considerations focuses on what a teacher needs to
know about student readiness to access each lesson (reading demands,
background knowledge, writing/encoding demands), and Next steps ties each
lesson to the bigger arc rather than just previewing what's next.

One structural note: this arc shifts from Arc A's poem/sculpture (literary
texts) to an informational science text, so the PA Core codes below are
mostly from the Reading *Informational* Text family (CC.1.2.4) instead of
Reading Literature (CC.1.3.4) — same official Great Minds alignment guide,
different section of it.

## Lesson 7 — Wonder (pp. 78-87)

**1. PA Core Standards:** CC.1.1.4.D (phonics/word analysis), CC.1.2.4.F
(word/phrase meaning), CC.1.2.4.J (vocabulary acquisition), CC.1.2.4.K
(vocabulary strategies), CC.1.2.4.L (range of informational reading).

**2. Learning Goals:** Notice and wonder about *The Circulatory Story*
(write three notices + three questions); determine the meaning of
"circulatory system" using the root *circ*, the word *system*, and context
clues.
Why it matters: first lesson of a brand-new informational text, and it
establishes a root-word decoding strategy (*circ* = circle) that gets
reused repeatedly through this whole arc.

**3. Student considerations:** No fluency benchmark this lesson, but
decoding multisyllabic words via root/word-part analysis is required. No
background knowledge of the circulatory system is assumed — the point is
to notice cold. The real access gap is vocabulary: a student who can't yet
infer meaning from word parts will need direct support connecting "circ"
to "circle" (the lesson provides a word bank: circle, parts, work
together, blood). Glossary-use literacy (pp. 40-41) is assumed and may
need modeling for students unfamiliar with it.

**4. Instructional Plan (pp. 78-85):** Read — listen closely to pp. 2-14,
notice/wonder on a class chart. Respond — extend notice/wonder to text
features on pp. 14-39, use the glossary for unknown words. Second Respond
— define "circulatory system" using root + word-part strategy, then
compare to the official definition. Land — reflect on knowledge charts.

**5. Success Criteria:** Three genuine notices + three related questions;
a written definition referencing both the "circle" root meaning and the
"related parts working together" meaning of *system*.

**6. Next steps:** The same root-word strategy gets reused for "coronary"
(*cor/cour* = heart) in Lesson 12 and revisited directly in the
Responsive Teaching lesson (16) — this is a recurring vocabulary thread
across the whole arc, not a one-time skill. Today's notice/wonder
questions feed directly into the informational-outline work that starts
in Lesson 8.

## Lesson 8 — Organize (pp. 88-99)

**1. PA Core Standards:** CC.1.2.4.A (main idea), CC.1.2.4.B (cite
details/inference), CC.1.2.4.C (explain events/procedures), CC.1.1.4.D,
CC.1.2.4.L.

**2. Learning Goals:** Identify key details and a main idea for pp. 11-13
using an informational outline; analyze a writing model's organization
(write to explain how an informative essay is organized).

**3. Student considerations:** Distinguishing a detail from a main idea is
an inferential skill the lesson explicitly models before releasing
students — a student new to this will need that same modeling, not just
the practice task. One new multisyllabic word (*ventricle*) needs
syllable-type decoding support. No heart-anatomy background is assumed
going in; the lesson builds it from scratch, but a student with prior
anatomy misconceptions may need those addressed directly since the lesson
doesn't anticipate correcting them.

**4. Instructional Plan (pp. 88-97):** Read — read aloud pp. 2-13, model
annotating for key details and main idea via think-aloud on page 8, then
release students to pp. 9-10 with differentiated support levels. Respond —
independently read pp. 11-13, add key details + main idea to the outline.
Write — introduce vocabulary term *inform*, examine the On-Target and
Advanced Writing Models paragraph by paragraph.

**5. Success Criteria:** Correctly identified key details + main idea for
pp. 11-13 using only that section's information; correct paragraph-by-
paragraph explanation of how the writing model answered its prompt.

**6. Next steps:** The informational outline built here is the literal raw
material students turn into proof paragraphs 1 and 2 starting in Lesson
12 — this isn't comprehension practice for its own sake, it's collecting
essay content. Main-idea-finding is practiced again on a new text section
in Lesson 10, so a student struggling with it today needs support before
then, not a repeat of today's approach.

## Lesson 9 — Reveal (pp. 100-113)

**1. PA Core Standards:** CC.1.2.4.B, CC.1.2.4.C, CC.1.1.4.D, CC.1.2.4.F,
CC.1.2.4.G (diverse media/illustrations), CC.1.2.4.J, CC.1.2.4.L.

**2. Learning Goals:** Analyze similes and metaphors in the text (explain
how a metaphor helps readers understand how valves work); analyze the
Painted Essay® structure (explain how it supports the Writing Model's
organization).

**3. Student considerations:** First lesson to teach simile/metaphor as
*informational*-text tools rather than literary ones (as in Arc A's Lesson
4) — a student who only connects figurative language to poetry may need
that bridge made explicit. Assumes the "figurative" vocabulary from Arc A
as a prerequisite; it's reviewed briefly, not retaught. The writing-model
color-coding task assumes the routine from Lesson 8 — a student who missed
that groundwork needs it retaught before this lesson's task makes sense.

**4. Instructional Plan (pp. 100-111):** Read — Repeated Reading routine
on page 9 (simile: heart = divided highway, with a "Highway" video),
page 10 (metaphor: chambers = rooms in a house), page 11 (simile: heart =
Grand Central Station, with two more videos). Respond — page 12-13,
identify the valve metaphor, journal-explain it. Write — examine the
full-color Painted Essay®, color-code the class Writing Model's five
paragraphs.

**5. Success Criteria:** Written explanation of specifically how the valve
metaphor clarifies one-way blood flow; correctly color-coded essay
structure matching the model.

**6. Next steps:** The five-part Painted Essay® structure examined here is
exactly what students use to draft their own Module Task 1 essay starting
in Lesson 12 — this is essay-structure rehearsal, not just text analysis.
The simile/metaphor-identification skill gets applied to a new topic
(coronary circuit) in Lesson 12.

## Lesson 10 — Organize (pp. 114-125)

**1. PA Core Standards:** CC.1.2.4.A, CC.1.2.4.B, CC.1.2.4.C, CC.1.1.4.D,
CC.1.2.4.J, CC.1.2.4.L.

**2. Learning Goals:** Describe blood vessel function (summarize pp. 14-22
using main ideas about arteries, capillaries, and veins); analyze proof
paragraphs (explain the connection between evidence and elaboration).

**3. Student considerations:** Three new vocabulary terms (artery,
capillary, vein) each need both syllable-decoding and a conceptual
distinction — a student who conflates them needs the compare/contrast
made explicit, since the text introduces all three back-to-back. The
outline task assumes fluency with Lesson 8's process, now across three
separate text sections in one lesson rather than one — a real jump in
density.

**4. Instructional Plan (pp. 114-123):** Read — reread pp. 14-22 in three
chunks (arteries, capillaries, veins), each paired with a syllable-type
decode of its term and a main-idea Think-Pair-Share. Respond — write a
summary of the whole section using all three terms correctly. Write —
label evidence vs. elaboration sentences in both proof paragraphs of the
writing model.

**5. Success Criteria:** A summary correctly using all three vessel terms
in their proper functional roles; correctly labeled evidence/elaboration
in both proof paragraphs.

**6. Next steps:** The evidence/elaboration labeling practiced here on the
model is exactly what students apply to their OWN evidence in Lesson 13 —
today is analysis, Lesson 13 is production of the same skill. The
three-vessel-type content is assumed background for Lesson 11's
illustration-based deep dive.

## Lesson 11 — Reveal (pp. 126-137)

**1. PA Core Standards:** CC.1.2.4.B, CC.1.2.4.C, CC.1.1.4.D, CC.1.2.4.G,
CC.1.2.4.L.

**2. Learning Goals:** Examine how illustrations explain information
(explain how illustrations support understanding of cellular respiration);
use transition words/phrases in informative writing.

**3. Student considerations:** No new reading vocabulary beyond "cellular
respiration" (taught via an illustrated metaphor), so the real access
point is visual literacy — can a student read a diagram for information
the way they'd read text? A student who treats illustrations as
decorative will need the modeled think-aloud (walking through what
arrows, labels, and branch points mean) more than most lessons provide.
The transition-words task assumes students distinguish connecting ideas
*within* a paragraph from connecting *across* paragraphs — the lesson
explicitly separates these.

**4. Instructional Plan (pp. 126-135):** Read — analyze illustrations
pp. 16-17 (subway simile, paired with a real DC Metro map) and pp. 18-19
(toll-booth simile for capillary narrowing). Respond — explain how the
"little green creature" illustration explains cellular respiration.
Write — identify transitions in the Writing Model, build a class
Transitions chart (four categories), revise own writing to add two.

**5. Success Criteria:** Explanation naming a specific way illustration
supports understanding, correctly applied to cellular respiration; revised
writing with two correctly-used transitions signaling the right kind of
connection.

**6. Next steps:** The four-category transitions chart becomes a standing
resource reused through the rest of the arc's writing tasks (12-14) — not
a one-lesson skill. The illustration-reading skill is tested again in
Lesson 12, applied to heart-health rather than cellular respiration.

## Lesson 12 — Reveal (pp. 138-149)

**1. PA Core Standards:** CC.1.2.4.B, CC.1.2.4.C, CC.1.1.4.D, CC.1.2.4.G,
CC.1.2.4.J, CC.1.2.4.L, CC.1.4.4.A (informative writing).

**2. Learning Goals:** Examine how text and illustrations work together
(pp. 36-37, on keeping the heart healthy); for Module Task 1, collect
evidence about why the author uses similes/metaphors.

**3. Student considerations:** Introduces "coronary" via the exact same
root/suffix strategy from Lesson 7 (*cor/cour* = heart, *-ary* = relating
to) — a student who never solidified that strategy will struggle with a
new instance rather than getting a fresh start. Two vocabulary words
(healthy, plaque) carry real health-content weight; a student without
prior health-education background may need the personified
"unhealthy-food-as-bad-character" illustration explained literally. The
evidence-collection task assumes students can locate similes/metaphors
across FIVE prior page ranges (pp. 5, 10, 11, 12-13, 16-17) — a student
without solid annotations from those lessons will need them re-pointed-to,
not expected to recall independently.

**4. Instructional Plan (pp. 138-147):** Read — root/suffix analysis of
"coronary," Repeated Reading of page 36's "clogged pipe" metaphor, define
"plaque." Respond — explain how text+illustrations together teach
heart-healthy habits; Whip Around sharing one healthy habit. Write — first
introduction of the Module Task 1 prompt this arc, examine provided
intro/conclusion paragraphs, introduce the 4-column Evidence Organizer,
locate similes/metaphors across the five page ranges.

**5. Success Criteria:** Explanation connecting both word choice
(personification) and illustration to the health main idea; a populated
Evidence Organizer citing at least four real similes/metaphors with
accurate sources.

**6. Next steps:** Today's Evidence Organizer is the literal source
material for the proof paragraphs elaborated in Lesson 13 and drafted in
Lesson 14 — nothing here is practice for its own sake. The same
evidence-organizer process reappears for Module Task 3 in Lesson 25.

## Lesson 13 — Distill (pp. 150-161)

**1. PA Core Standards:** CC.1.1.4.D, CC.1.2.4.A, CC.1.2.4.B, CC.1.2.4.F,
CC.1.2.4.J, CC.1.2.4.L, CC.1.4.4.A, CC.1.4.4.C (writing content).

**2. Learning Goals:** Determine a central idea (share a big idea via
class discussion); use elaboration to develop evidence (elaborate on four
pieces of evidence for Module Task 1).

**3. Student considerations:** The central-idea discussion is a jigsaw
across seven page-range groups — a student needs to understand their own
chunk AND synthesize peers' findings for chunks they didn't read; a
student strong in reading but weaker in listening comprehension may follow
their own group but miss the whole-text synthesis. The writing demand
jumps here: four separate elaboration sentences (not one), each connecting
a specific piece of evidence to a specific thesis point — a real volume
increase from Lessons 9-12's single-example tasks.

**4. Instructional Plan (pp. 150-159):** Read — seven small groups each
examine a different page range, share findings in text order, introduce
vocabulary "circulate." Respond — discussion using a Talking Tool for
citing evidence, write an individual central-idea statement. Write — model
elaborating on one piece of evidence, then independently elaborate on the
remaining three.

**5. Success Criteria:** A central-idea statement synthesizing the whole
text, not just one group's section; four elaboration sentences each
correctly connecting evidence to its supporting point.

**6. Next steps:** Today's central idea and four elaborated sentences are
the direct raw material for the two full proof paragraphs drafted in
Lesson 14 — today produces the pieces, Lesson 14 assembles them.

## Lesson 14 — Know (pp. 162-173)

**1. PA Core Standards:** CC.1.2.4.B, CC.1.2.4.J, CC.1.2.4.L, CC.1.4.4.A,
CC.1.4.4.C, CC.1.4.4.D (organization), CC.1.4.4.E (style), CC.1.4.4.F
(conventions) — the heaviest writing-standards lesson in the arc.
Notably, no CC.1.1.4.D — this lesson has no new decoding demand.

**2. Learning Goals:** Reflect on knowledge gained (write two knowledge
statements using coordinating conjunctions); for Module Task 1, draft
proof paragraphs 1 and 2 using the thesis as a guide.

**3. Student considerations:** Coordinating conjunctions (and/but/or)
require a student to already write complete sentences (the Arc A, Lesson
6 skill) before combining two of them meaningfully — a student still
writing fragments needs that prerequisite addressed first. The
proof-paragraph drafting task is the single largest independent-writing
demand in the arc so far — two full paragraphs each needing a topic
sentence, evidence, and elaboration in order — a real jump from the
journal-length writing of Lessons 7-13 that may need the provided
margin-labeling scaffold, not the thesis alone.

**4. Instructional Plan (pp. 162-171):** Read — review the World
Knowledge Chart. Respond — introduce coordinating conjunctions via a card
game expanding/linking chart statements, write two new knowledge
statements. Write — review the Evidence Organizer and Checklist, orally
rehearse topic sentences with a partner, draft both proof paragraphs.

**5. Success Criteria:** Two knowledge statements correctly using a
coordinating conjunction; two drafted proof paragraphs each with a topic
sentence, evidence, and elaboration in order.

**6. Next steps:** These are first drafts — Lesson 15 immediately revises
this same writing with a checklist, and Lesson 16 has no independent
writing task at all. Today's output directly determines how much revision
each student actually needs in Lesson 15.

## Lesson 15 — Reading Comprehension Assessment 1 (pp. 174-179)

**1. PA Core Standards:** CC.1.4.4.A, CC.1.4.4.C, CC.1.4.4.E — writing
only. No new reading standard; this is an assessment day.

**2. Learning Goals:** Demonstrate knowledge of the heart and apply
reading comprehension skills to a new text (complete Reading Comprehension
Assessment 1); revise proof paragraphs 1 and 2 using a checklist.

**3. Student considerations:** This is the arc's first formal, timed,
four-part assessment (fluency read-aloud, vocabulary questions, close
reading of a brand-new text, self-evaluation) — a student who hasn't been
taught how to approach unfamiliar text under assessment conditions (versus
the heavily-scaffolded reads used every lesson through 14) may struggle
with the format itself, separate from content knowledge. The revision task
requires precise vocabulary substitution (swapping "parts" for
"chambers") — a student with a thin topic-specific vocabulary bank may
struggle here on word choice specifically, not understanding.

**4. Instructional Plan (pp. 174-178):** Respond — administer the
four-section assessment; teacher uses real performance data to plan
Lesson 16. Write — model swapping a general term for a topic-specific one
per the checklist, students apply it to their own draft with a partner.

**5. Success Criteria:** Assessment performance scored via the Assessment
Guide; a revised draft with general terms correctly replaced by
topic-specific vocabulary.

**6. Next steps:** This assessment's real results directly determine what
gets taught in Lesson 16 — that lesson isn't generic review, it's built
around exactly what this one reveals. The revised draft becomes the final
submitted Module Task 1.

## Lesson 16 — Responsive Teaching (pp. 180-185)

**1. PA Core Standards:** CC.1.1.4.D, CC.1.2.4.B, CC.1.2.4.J, CC.1.2.4.K,
CC.1.2.4.L. No writing standard — no Write section this lesson.

**2. Learning Goal:** Analyze relevant assessment questions (explain how
to identify correct answers).

**3. Student considerations:** This lesson's actual content is determined
by Lesson 15's real results — the teacher chooses which questions to
review and which vocabulary activities to run based on real performance
data, so there's no fixed content beyond knowing your own class's missed
items going in. The vocabulary activity reuses the "circ" root work a
third time (after Lessons 7 and 12) — a student who still hasn't
internalized it needs this to be the explicit, called-out moment.

**4. Instructional Plan (pp. 180-184):** Read — reread the assessment text
aloud. Respond — discuss selected (data-driven) questions as a class.
Engage — vocabulary consolidation via a Word Parts Web for "circ," or
Knowledge Card review, chosen based on which terms the assessment showed
as weak.

**5. Success Criteria:** Not a fixed product — success is the class
correctly re-deriving answers to their own previously-missed questions,
and a richer set of circ-root vocabulary than Lessons 7 or 12 produced
alone.

**6. Next steps:** This closes Arc B. Arc C shifts to a new anchor text
(*The Gross Clinic*, a painting) and back to visual-art/literary analysis
rather than informational science reading — the root-word vocabulary work
doesn't carry forward directly, but the Painted Essay® structure and
Evidence Organizer process absolutely do; Module Task 2 in Arc C reuses
the exact same essay-drafting machinery just practiced here.

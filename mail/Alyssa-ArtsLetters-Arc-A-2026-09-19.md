## Lesson 1 — Opening Bookend (Teach Book pp. 14-19)

**1. PA Core Standards:** This is an activation/hook lesson, not a
standards-driven content lesson — it's genuinely light by design (2-minute
launch, teacher's choice of engagement activity). If you want a standard to
anchor it for documentation purposes, CC.1.5.4 (speaking and listening —
sharing experiences, building on others' ideas) fits the "Share: Discuss
Prior Knowledge" segment loosely, but I wouldn't force a heavier citation
onto what's honestly a warm-up.

**2. Learning Goals / I can statements:**
- I can share what I already know or have experienced about having a great heart.
- I can explore the topic of our new module.
Why it matters: this lesson exists to surface prior knowledge and personal
connection *before* any text work starts, so students have a stake in the
Essential Question ("What does having a great heart mean?") before they're
asked to analyze anything.

**3. Student considerations:** The Teach Book explicitly hands you the
engagement activity as an open choice — drawing, experiencing a beating
heart, reading a text about someone with a great heart, or a teacher-created
experience — with a Teacher Note telling you to customize it to your
students' regional geography, community resources, and funds of knowledge
(home life, language, experiences). Practically: this is your one chance all
module to make the topic *local* — a Pittsburgh-specific angle (a community
member known for generosity, a local health/EMS tie-in to the literal heart)
would do real work here for a class that skews toward local relevance.

**4. Instructional Plan (p. 14-15):** Launch (2 min) — choral-read the
Essential Question. Learn — Share: discuss prior knowledge about a great
heart; Engage: your chosen activity. Land — revisit the Essential Question.
No assigned text, no annotation, no formal facilitation strategy beyond your
own choice of activity — the rigor comes later; this lesson's job is buy-in.

**5. Success Criteria / Evidence of learning:** Informal only — the Teach
Book gives no rubric or task here. Evidence would be participation quality:
are students actually offering real experiences/ideas about "great heart,"
or just complying? Worth a quick anecdotal note for yourself on who has
background knowledge to draw on later and who doesn't.

**6. Next steps:** Lesson 2 begins the actual text work — first read of
"Heart to Heart" and the first look at *Mother and Child*.

## Lesson 2 — Wonder (Teach Book pp. 20-31)

**1. PA Core Standards:** CC.1.1.4 (foundational skills — reading with
accuracy) for the fluency task; CC.1.3.4 (reading literature — initial
observation/questioning of a text) for the Notice-and-Wonder work; a visual
arts observation standard (outside core ELA, likely your building's arts
integration standard) for the *Mother and Child* half.

**2. Learning Goals / I can statements:**
- I can read "Heart to Heart" with accuracy, decoding each word correctly.
- I can notice and wonder about a work of art — write at least two things I
  notice about *Mother and Child* and two related questions.
Why it matters: this is the module's first real encounter with both anchor
texts. Accuracy-first fluency and open noticing/wondering are deliberately
low-stakes — the goal is curiosity and a clean first read, not analysis yet.

**3. Student considerations (p. 23-24):** The lesson explicitly models
annotation ("annotate the word *regret*... share with a partner a word they
annotated") and gives you a differentiation challenge (have advanced
students generate synonyms for *yearn* and build sentences) and a language
support (pause to define *clutch*, *mute*, *tattoo* with synonyms for
students who need it). The Teacher Note also flags this as an informal
*formative assessment* moment — watching whether students already use
"poem," "stanza," "line," "speaker" correctly from prior grades. Worth
actually tracking that; it tells you who needs vocabulary front-loaded
before Lesson 3's fluency push.

**4. Instructional Plan (p. 20-24):** Launch (4 min) — orient to the
Essential Question and the year's routine. Learn (Read, 13 min: silent read
+ annotate unknown words, modeled think-aloud, Notice/Wonder chart, partner
share; Observe: same Notice/Wonder routine applied to the sculpture). Land —
reflect. Facilitation leans on modeling (teacher think-aloud shown verbatim
in the book) before releasing to partners — a gradual-release structure,
not a straight lecture.

**5. Success Criteria / Evidence of learning:** Two genuine notices + two
related questions on the Notice and Wonder Chart, for both the poem and the
sculpture. Accuracy in the read-aloud (not fluency yet — that's explicitly
lessons 3-6's job).

**6. Next steps:** Lesson 3 moves from noticing to literal comprehension —
organizing what's actually happening in the poem, stanza by stanza.

## Lesson 3 — Organize (Teach Book pp. 32-45)

**1. PA Core Standards:** CC.1.1.4 (fluency — phrasing/pausing for
punctuation) and CC.1.3.4 (literal comprehension — identifying what's
happening in a text, describing elements of a visual work).

**2. Learning Goals / I can statements:**
- I can read "Heart to Heart" with appropriate phrasing, grouping words into
  phrases and pausing for punctuation.
- I can describe the forms within *Mother and Child*.
Why it matters: phrasing is the fluency skill that turns decoding into
actual meaning-making — a student who reads word-by-word can't yet organize
a poem's ideas. This lesson makes that link explicit before asking for any
interpretation.

**3. Student considerations (p. 32):** A Prologue lesson exists for this one
specifically for students needing additional support — worth flagging for
any multilingual learners or students with language-based needs in your
room, since it's built for exactly that. New vocabulary (*abstract art*,
*form*, *heart* as a noun) gets introduced here too, ahead of the more
figurative-language-heavy Lesson 4.

**4. Instructional Plan (p. 32-33):** Read: model + practice appropriate
phrasing in "Heart to Heart." Respond: organize the poem's elements using a
class Poem Organizer (stanza-by-stanza, "what is happening" style —
genuinely useful as a template you could reuse for other poems). Observe:
describe the sculpture's forms. Land: view "Poet Laureate" video (Rita Dove
herself) — a nice authentic hook tying the poet to her own reading of craft.

**5. Success Criteria / Evidence of learning:** A completed Poem Organizer
showing literal, stanza-level understanding; correctly phrased oral reading;
a written description of the sculpture's forms.

**6. Next steps:** Lesson 4 is where the module's real thesis work starts —
literal vs. figurative meaning of "heart," which is the whole Essential
Question in miniature.

## Lesson 4 — Reveal (Teach Book pp. 46-57)

**1. PA Core Standards:** CC.1.3.4 (reading literature — determining the
meaning of words and phrases as they're used in a text, including
figurative language) is the clear center of this lesson; CC.1.1.4 (fluency —
expression) for the read-aloud goal.

**2. Learning Goals / I can statements:**
- I can read "Heart to Heart" with expression, using voice to reflect
  feeling.
- I can explain the effects of negative space in *Mother and Child*.
Why it matters: this is the pivot lesson for the whole module's Essential
Question. Vocabulary introduced here — *figurative*, *literal*, *idiom*,
*negative space* — is the actual conceptual payload of "A Great Heart," and
it's taught side-by-side across the poem AND the sculpture, which is a
genuinely strong design choice (the same literal/figurative lens applied to
two different media).

**3. Student considerations (p. 46-47):** Materials include a Syllable
Types reference chart and side-by-side display of *literal* and *figurative*
with syllable divisions — a decoding scaffold layered onto a vocabulary
lesson, useful for students still building multisyllabic word-attack
skills. A Prologue supports students who need help distinguishing literal
from figurative reference specifically (a genuinely hard distinction at
this age even without a language barrier).

**4. Instructional Plan (p. 46-47):** Read: expressive read-aloud of "Heart
to Heart." Respond: examine the poem's literal vs. figurative language
using an idiom chart. Observe: examine negative space in the sculpture and
its effect on viewer interpretation. The parallel structure (same concept,
two media) is the instructional strategy worth naming explicitly if an
evaluator asks "how does this lesson promote rigor" — it's transfer, not
repetition.

**5. Success Criteria / Evidence of learning:** Correctly completed Idiom
Chart distinguishing literal/figurative heart references; a written
explanation of negative space's effect, using the term correctly.

**6. Next steps:** Lesson 5 synthesizes — moving from identifying
literal/figurative language to stating a central idea about what the works
communicate.

## Lesson 5 — Distill (Teach Book pp. 58-69)

**1. PA Core Standards:** CC.1.1.4 (fluency — appropriate rate) and CC.1.3.4
(determining central idea/theme through discussion, supporting claims about
a text).

**2. Learning Goals / I can statements:**
- I can read "Heart to Heart" fluently at an appropriate rate.
- I can, during a class discussion, explain what *Mother and Child*
  conveys about the relationship between a mother and child.
Why it matters: this is the first lesson asking students to make an
interpretive CLAIM (a central idea) rather than describe or organize —
genuinely the biggest cognitive jump in the arc so far.

**3. Student considerations (p. 58):** No new vocabulary and no Prologue
this time — by design, this is a discussion-and-synthesis lesson, not a
new-content lesson, so it's a lighter cognitive-load day on the *input*
side even though the thinking asked of students is harder. Worth watching
which students go quiet during a central-idea discussion versus the earlier
literal-comprehension lessons — that's your signal for who needs more
scaffolding on inference specifically, not decoding.

**4. Instructional Plan (p. 58-59):** Read: fluent practice read. Respond:
class discussion distilling a central idea from "Heart to Heart." Observe:
class discussion on what the sculpture communicates about motherhood. Both
are discussion-based, not written-response-based — an oral rehearsal for
the written synthesis coming in Lesson 6.

**5. Success Criteria / Evidence of learning:** Participation in a
substantive discussion that names and defends a central idea with reference
to the text/artwork — informal, discussion-based evidence rather than a
worksheet.

**6. Next steps:** Lesson 6 closes the arc — full synthesis in writing,
setting up the transition into Arc B ("The Circulatory Story").

## Lesson 6 — Know (Teach Book pp. 70-77)

**1. PA Core Standards:** CC.1.1.4 (fluency — performance-level reading)
and CC.1.4.4 (writing — expressing knowledge gained in complete sentences,
informative writing groundwork).

**2. Learning Goals / I can statements:**
- I can perform a fluent reading of "Heart to Heart" for a small group.
- I can write complete sentences to express knowledge gained from
  studying *Mother and Child*.
Why it matters: this closes the Wonder→Organize→Reveal→Distill→Know cycle —
students go from first noticing the text (L2) to performing it and writing
their own synthesis of it (L6), which is the module's whole instructional
model in miniature and a good one to point to if you're asked to justify
this curriculum's design to an administrator.

**3. Student considerations (p. 70-71):** The performance element (reading
aloud "for a small group") is a genuine speaking/listening opportunity and
a mild public-speaking stretch for students who find that hard — worth
pre-thinking your grouping here rather than leaving it random. The writing
task explicitly requires complete sentences, so this doubles as a light
conventions check.

**4. Instructional Plan (p. 70-71):** Prepare charts on literal/figurative
language, hearts, Rita Dove, and poet laureates around the room ahead of
time (listed under Preparation). Read: small-group fluent performance.
Respond/Observe: write complete sentences expressing knowledge gained from
each text. Land: synthesize learning — the arc's actual closing move.

**5. Success Criteria / Evidence of learning:** A fluent small-group
performance; written complete sentences that genuinely reflect knowledge
gained (not just restating the text) about both the poem and the sculpture.

**6. Next steps:** Arc B begins — "The Circulatory Story," moving from the
figurative heart to the literal, biological one, which is where the
module's science-adjacent informational reading starts.

LESSON 1 — Opening Bookend (pp. 14-19)

1. PA Core Standards

None assigned. Great Minds' PA alignment guide does not code Lesson 1 to any standard. Like the Module 1 opener, it is an activation lesson (a short launch, a discussion of prior knowledge, then a teacher's-choice engagement activity), not a content-bearing lesson.

2. Learning Goals / I can statements

- I can share experiences about enduring stories.
- I can explore the module topic.

Why it matters: Students meet the Essential Question ("What do people learn from myths and stories?") and the word endure through their own family and community stories, so they have personal examples of stories that last before they read a single myth.

3. Student considerations

There is no assigned text, so there is no decoding demand. Access is oral: students Think-Pair-Share about a favorite story from their family or community. Some students may not have a family story they are comfortable sharing; let a community story, a book, or a movie count so no one is put on the spot. Students who come from strong storytelling traditions, or who speak another home language, are an asset here, and the Teach Book asks you to honor home life, languages, and experiences. The Knowledge Card for endure carries two meanings (to last a long time; to put up with something hard). The module leans on the first, so name that when you introduce it. Depending on the Engage option you choose, plan for more than 60 minutes.

4. Learning Progress / Instructional Plan (pp. 15-18)

Launch (5 min): introduce endure with the Knowledge Card and Vocabulary Exploration, then choral-read the Essential Question. Learn (50 min): Share (8 min), a Think-Pair-Share on "What is a favorite story shared in your family or community?" followed by a short discussion of why it is a favorite and how stories are shared. Engage (42 min), one option: (1) a community member or librarian shares a local story; (2) an artist or a live or recorded performance tells a story, then the class brainstorms other ways people share stories; (3) read an enduring story (a fable or folktale) from the volume of reading list and discuss whether its theme still matters today. Land (5 min): connect the experience to the Essential Question with the option-specific question. The rigor in this lesson is schema building and buy-in: students start the module holding their own examples of stories that last, which they will set against ancient myths.

5. Success Criteria / Evidence of learning

Informal only; there is no learning task or rubric.
- Students will share a story from their family or community and explain why it matters.
- Students will explain why a story gets passed down, or why its message still matters today.
Keep a quick note of who already knows myths or folktales and of any misconceptions; the Teach Book asks you to note both for later discussions.

6. Next steps

Lesson 2 defines a myth as a special kind of story and introduces Gifts from the Gods. Today's examples make a natural bridge: a family story may last a generation, and Greek myths have lasted thousands of years. Why? Bring your prior-knowledge notes to the World and ELA Knowledge Charts that start in Lesson 2.

Lesson 2 is day 1 of the four-day fluency cycle (accuracy), which is where the fluency codes come from.

2. Learning Goals / I can statements

- I can describe what makes a myth unique, using excerpts from Understanding Greek Myths.
- I can notice and wonder about Gifts from the Gods.

Why it matters: The class What Are Myths? list built today (gods, heroes and monsters; ancient and supernatural settings; passed down through generations; creation myths and morality myths) is the reference students use on the myth organizers in Lessons 3 and 4 and through the end of the arc.

3. Student considerations

Students handle two texts in one lesson: the excerpts from Understanding Greek Myths (read aloud) and the covers and introduction of Gifts from the Gods. The excerpts carry domain words (supernatural, domain, generation, morality); the Teach Book suggests pausing to define them with a synonym. Students with little exposure to Greek mythology will not have the names or the difference between gods, heroes, and humans, so the What Are Myths? list is their scaffold; keep it posted. Gifts from the Gods is partially unpaginated: number the pages before the lesson (title page as i, "Achilles' Heel" as page 1), or students will not be able to follow page references later in the arc. For the accuracy work, students annotate words that may be hard to decode; students still building multisyllabic decoding benefit from seeing you use phonics on their annotated words (the Teach Book suggests this if time allows).

4. Learning Progress / Instructional Plan (pp. 22-27)

Launch (2 min): display the excerpts and choral-read the Content Framing Question. Learn (53 min): Read 1 (21 min), a Think-Pair-Share on the word story and its Knowledge Card; read aloud the excerpts (Learn book p. 7) while students annotate details that describe myths; partners share annotations; build the What Are Myths? list; introduce myth (clap the syllables, infer its meaning from the list, confirm with the Knowledge Card); learning task: a journal response to "What makes a story a myth?" Read 2 (13 min), discuss what readers do when they notice and wonder, examine the covers, read the back-cover description, then read aloud pp. ii-iii while students annotate and add to the class Notice and Wonder Chart. Respond (19 min), learning task: at least three notices and three related questions (Learn book p. 9), then each student picks the question they most want answered; fluency: review the Fluency Reference Chart, focus on accuracy, annotate challenging words in the passage (Learn book p. 89), watch you model a mistake and self-correct, then read with a partner. Land (5 min): introduce the World Knowledge and ELA Knowledge Charts and add a few student knowledge statements. Rigor comes from building a definition out of evidence (list, then inferred meaning, then the Knowledge Card) instead of receiving one, and from requiring each question to connect to something the student noticed.

5. Success Criteria / Evidence of learning

- Students will write a journal response explaining what makes a story a myth, using details from the excerpts (types of characters, ancient settings, being passed down, creation and morality types).
- Students will write at least three things they notice about Gifts from the Gods and at least three questions connected to those observations.
- Students will read the fluency passage to a partner with attention to accuracy and self-correct small mistakes.

6. Next steps

Lesson 3 applies today's definition right away: students read the myth about Nemesis, sort its elements on Myth Organizer 1, and decide whether it is a creation or a morality myth. Students who could not name the two types today need the What Are Myths? list in hand. The notice-and-wonder routine returns with The Invention of Hugo Cabret in Lesson 8, and fluency continues tomorrow with phrasing on the same passage.

CC.1.3.4.G (connecting a story to a visual version of it) matches the end of the Respond section, where students choose the illustration on pp. 54-57 that best summarizes the myth.

2. Learning Goals / I can statements

- I can identify elements of a myth in Gifts from the Gods.
- I can notice and wonder about Winged Victory.

Why it matters: This is the first full myth students read for its structure (characters, setting, events, what it explains) and their first written summary. It also introduces Winged Victory, the work of art that runs through Lesson 7.

3. Student considerations

Partners read the Nemesis myth (p. 54) on their own, and it contains words such as braggarts, dispense, and transgressions; the Teach Book suggests defining them with a synonym. The syllable work on nemesis and tantalize (nem-es-is, tan-tal-ize) uses the Syllable Types chart; a student who produces the wrong vowel sound needs the prompt "What other sound could this vowel make?" A Prologue to Lesson 3 is available for students who need additional support, so decide in advance who will get it. The summary is scaffolded with sentence frames (This myth is about..., It takes place in..., First/Then/Last..., This myth explains...); students who find writing hard should rehearse the summary orally first, which is the Teach Book's own support. For Winged Victory, the Teach Book deliberately gives no background yet. Some students will want "the answer," so set the expectation that wondering is the task. Students who have never seen ancient sculpture may not realize the missing head and arms are damage, not design; let that come up as a wonder.

4. Learning Progress / Instructional Plan (pp. 32-40)

Launch (2 min): choral-read the Content Framing Question and explain the Organize lesson. Learn (53 min): Read (20 min), phrasing on the Fluency Reference Chart, annotating pauses with slash marks, and partner reading; read the sentence on p. iii about how myths endured; look at the names on the cover scroll; decode nemesis and tantalize; introduce Myth Organizer 1 (Learn book p. 10); partners read and annotate the Nemesis myth; the class completes the organizer together (characters: Nemesis, Croesus, Solon; setting; events; what it explains: do not be too proud or brag) and decides it is a morality myth. Respond (18 min), a think-aloud with the sentence frames, then the learning task: a written summary in the journal, read aloud to a partner, and a choice of the illustration that best summarizes the myth. Observe (15 min), two minutes of silent observation of Winged Victory, a Think-Pair-Share, the class chart, then the learning task: three notices and three questions (Learn book p. 11), starring one question to return to. Land (5 min): knowledge charts. Rigor builds in steps: text evidence, then the organizer, then a summary (synthesis), and the creation-or-morality call must come with a reason. Choosing and defending the best illustration is an evaluation task.

5. Success Criteria / Evidence of learning

- Students will complete Myth Organizer 1 with the characters, setting, events, and what the Nemesis myth explains.
- Students will write a summary of the Nemesis myth that includes the characters, setting, events, and the lesson it teaches.
- Students will classify the myth as a morality myth and give a reason.
- Students will write at least three things they notice about Winged Victory and at least three related questions.

6. Next steps

Lesson 4 repeats the organizer with a new myth in a Jigsaw and moves from a written summary to an oral one, so today's summary is the model. Students whose summaries were missing elements should keep Organizer 1 out as a reference. The starred Winged Victory questions come back in Lessons 4-6, when students learn the sculpture's history, and fluency moves on to expression.

CC.1.3.4.H (comparing themes and patterns across stories) lines up with the journal task comparing what two myths explain about people, and CC.1.5.4.D (recounting in an organized manner) with the oral summary.

2. Learning Goals / I can statements

- I can summarize the elements of a myth from Gifts from the Gods.
- I can describe Winged Victory.

Why it matters: Students apply the myth organizer in groups and then teach their myth to classmates who have not read it. It is the first time each student is responsible for content the rest of the group depends on.

3. Student considerations

The Jigsaw uses five myths of different lengths: "Achilles' Heel" (pp. 2-5), "Fortuna" (pp. 24-25), "Muses" (pp. 50-51), "Pandora's Box" (pp. 60-64, without the sidebar), and "Victoria" (pp. 80-81). Pandora's Box is the longest, so match assignments to reading levels; the Teach Book recommends strategic grouping, including pairing students who share a home language. "Fortuna" is the only Roman myth, so a map of Greece and Rome helps. The Teach Book's key calls some myths creation myths in ways students may argue with (for example, "Achilles' Heel" counts as a creation myth because it explains where human weakness comes from). Welcome the argument; what matters is a reason, not matching the key. The oral summary means speaking in a group of five, so keep the sentence frames up for students who need language support. For the Winged Victory description, the "what looks real, what looks imaginary" question gives a concrete entry point. A Prologue to Lesson 4 is available.

4. Learning Progress / Instructional Plan (pp. 44-53)

Launch (2 min). Learn (53 min): Read (20 min), the fluency focus on expression (you read one sentence with expression, students name how you used your voice, annotate the passage, and read with a partner); discuss what people can learn from myths; introduce Myth Organizer 2 (Learn book p. 12); Jigsaw groups read their assigned myth, complete the organizer, and choose an illustration that summarizes it. Respond (18 min), the learning task: in mixed groups of five (one from each Jigsaw group), each student gives an oral summary and shows the illustration; then a journal entry comparing and contrasting what their myth and one other myth explain about people. Observe (15 min), silent observation of Winged Victory; a Think-Pair-Share on the figure's pose; background (found in 1863 on Samothrace; originally had a head and arms); students imitate the pose; discuss the drapery, the base (the front of a ship), and what looks real or imaginary; learning task: a paragraph describing the sculpture to a friend who is not in the room. Land (5 min): knowledge charts. Rigor comes from the Jigsaw, where each student is the expert; the compare-and-contrast entry moves students from summary to analysis of what myths say about people; and the descriptive paragraph demands precise detail for a reader who cannot see the work.

5. Success Criteria / Evidence of learning

- Students will complete Myth Organizer 2 for their assigned myth.
- Students will give an oral summary of their myth that includes the characters, setting, events, what the myth explains, and whether it is a creation or a morality myth.
- Students will write a comparison of what two myths explain about people.
- Students will write a paragraph describing Winged Victory with specific details (pose, drapery, wings, base).

6. Next steps

Lesson 5 returns to three of these myths (Nemesis, Fortuna, Victoria) to study how their names became English words, so every student needs to have heard the summaries; anyone absent today should read pp. 24-25 and 80-81 before Lesson 5. Students summarize again with The Invention of Hugo Cabret in Lesson 9. Today's sculpture details (the folds of the robe, the ship's base) are the raw material for the study of line in Lesson 5.

The vocabulary codes (CC.1.2.4.J and CC.1.3.4.I) carry this lesson: it is built around word history.

2. Learning Goals / I can statements

- I can explain how the etymology of a word helps readers better understand new texts.
- I can examine the use of lines in Winged Victory.

Why it matters: This is where the arc turns from myths as stories to myths as the source of everyday English words (nemesis, fortune, victory), which is one of the module's knowledge threads.

3. Student considerations

The vocabulary load is high: four Knowledge Cards (etymology, nemesis, fortune, victory) plus words the Teach Book suggests defining on the spot (retribution, vengeance, destiny, triumph). It deliberately trims the book's definitions to keep the load manageable; follow that lead. Students need to remember the Nemesis, Fortuna, and Victoria myths from Lessons 3-4, and the lesson rereads each one. The modern excerpts come from novels (Lemony Snicket: A Series of Unfortunate Events, Emily's Runaway Imagination, The Battle for Skandia); students who know those books connect quickly, while others need a sentence of context. The Teach Book flags two errors in the text: the title on p. 79 should read Skandia, and the dates in the last sentence on p. 58 are when King Croesus reigned. For the art work, students sketch the lines they see; reluctant artists should hear that the sketch is a thinking tool, not an art grade. The Teach Book suggests letting students who share a home language talk through the line questions together first.

4. Learning Progress / Instructional Plan (pp. 58-67)

Launch (5 min): introduce etymology with its Knowledge Card, choral-read the Content Framing Question, and explain the Reveal lesson. Learn (50 min): Read (23 min), rate on the Fluency Reference Chart, Echo Read, and a partner reading that uses all four fluency elements (the last day of explicit fluency instruction); read the sentences on p. iii about words with stories; nemesis Knowledge Card, reread pp. 54-57, and discuss why people began to dislike Nemesis; read the Lemony Snicket excerpt on p. 53 and discuss why a nemesis might be the worst enemy; fortune Knowledge Card, read pp. 24-25 and discuss why people prayed to Fortuna; read the excerpt on p. 23 and discuss why fortune means different things to different people. Respond (12 min), the victory Knowledge Card, read pp. 80-81 and the excerpt on p. 79, discuss why someone with many victories would not worry about defeat, then the learning task: a journal entry explaining how the etymology of victory deepens its meaning; close with evidence that words from myths endure today. Observe (15 min), line as an element of art and its types, three minutes of silent looking, sketching the lines, then where straight and curved lines appear and how they suggest movement, stillness, and strength; learning task: explain what the lines reveal about the sculpture. Land (5 min): knowledge charts. Rigor comes from explaining why a word means what it means (through its history) rather than memorizing a definition, and from inferring meaning (movement, strength) from a formal element of art.

5. Success Criteria / Evidence of learning

- Students will write how the myth of Victoria adds meaning to the word victory (for example, winning takes more than strength: the cause must be just and the fight fair).
- Students will use nemesis and fortune correctly in discussion and tie each word to its myth.
- Students will read the fluency passage at an appropriate rate with accuracy, phrasing, and expression.
- Students will write how the lines in Winged Victory suggest movement or strength, with specific examples.

6. Next steps

This is the last day of explicit fluency lessons. After Lesson 6, small groups rehearse and perform the passage, and from then on fluency is practiced in context. Watch for readers who still need regular support (Implementation Resources has goal-setting and tracking advice). Lesson 6 asks how the author of Gifts from the Gods uses evidence, including today's definitions and modern excerpts, to support a point, and Lesson 9 asks students to use myth terms in their own writing. Today's line work feeds Lesson 6's discussion question: How does the sculpture depict victory?

CC.1.2.4.H (how an author uses reasons and evidence to support points) is the heart of this lesson.

2. Learning Goals / I can statements

- I can analyze evidence in Gifts from the Gods.
- I can conclude what Winged Victory communicates about victory.

Why it matters: For the first time in the module, students read Gifts from the Gods as an informational text with an author's point and supporting evidence, not just as a collection of myths.

3. Student considerations

The lesson assumes students remember from Module 1 that illustrations can be evidence (The Circulatory Story); a student new to the class will not have that. The author's point is displayed for students (Myths can help people understand words and the world more deeply) after they discuss it; students who struggle to find a point in an introduction can use the Teach Book's questions about its last sentence. In the Gallery Walk, small groups read and discuss assigned pages on their own, so place groups with care: the Author's Note (pp. 84-85) is the densest prose, while the Myths station (pp. 24-25, 80-81) rereads familiar stories. The vocabulary term illuminate comes with the root lum/lumin ("light, glow"), which returns in Lesson 22. The class discussion uses the speaking and listening goal "Ask questions to deepen your understanding" and the Talking Tool (Learn book p. 35); a student who is hesitant to speak can start with a question frame instead of a claim. The victorious-pose activity is physical; a student who does not want to stand and pose can describe a pose instead. A Prologue to Lesson 6 is available.

4. Learning Progress / Instructional Plan (pp. 72-80)

Launch (2 min). Learn (53 min): Read (20 min), choral-read the introduction; introduce illuminate (Knowledge Card, clap the syllables, the root lum/lumin, infer the meaning); discuss the point the author makes on pp. ii-iii, then display it; name the types of evidence (illustrations, definitions, modern excerpts, myths, additional words, author's note); the class completes the Illustrations chart together (pp. 5, 12, 54), and small groups take the other five types with their assigned pages. Respond (15 min), a Gallery Walk, then the learning task: a journal response explaining how the author supports the point that myths help people understand words and the world more deeply. Observe (18 min), background on Winged Victory (marble, found in 1863 on Samothrace, set in a sanctuary facing the sea, about 18 feet tall); a nonverbal vote on whether it shows a goddess, a hero, or a human (it is the goddess Victoria; title: Winged Victory of Samothrace); students strike a victorious pose and the class lists traits of victory; small groups look for those traits in the sculpture; introduce the discussion question, the speaking and listening goal, and the Talking Tool; learning task: a class discussion of "How does the sculpture depict victory?" while you track progress on the Module 2 Speaking and Listening Goal Tracker. Land (5 min): knowledge charts. Rigor comes from evaluating how each type of evidence supports a claim, and from the discussion, which asks students to connect formal elements (size, marble, pose, line) to an abstract idea and to question each other's thinking.

5. Success Criteria / Evidence of learning

- Students will write how the author supports the point that myths help people understand words and the world, naming at least two types of evidence (for example, the definitions of genius and grace, the myths of Fortuna and Victoria, or modern excerpts that use echo and hypnotize).
- Students will add to a group chart explaining how one type of evidence supports the author's point.
- Students will explain in the class discussion how the sculpture depicts victory, using details (size, marble, the forward-leaning pose, lines), and ask at least one question that deepens a classmate's idea.

6. Next steps

Lesson 7 closes the arc by turning what students know into knowledge statements with adjectives, about both the myths and Winged Victory; today's list of victory traits (determined, strong, powerful, brave) is a ready source of adjectives. The roots lum and lumin come back in Lesson 22, and students examine evidence again in Module 3. The speaking and listening tracker started today runs all module, so note who needs more practice asking questions. Students return to how art conveys ideas with A Trip to the Moon in Lesson 34.

CC.1.4.4.R (conventions of standard English) comes from the adjective work in Sentence Strategy 4.

2. Learning Goals / I can statements

- I can reflect on the knowledge gained from studying Gifts from the Gods and "The Myth of Prometheus."
- I can describe the knowledge gained from studying Winged Victory.

Why it matters: This is the arc's synthesis lesson. It adds one more myth with both creation and morality elements, and it introduces adjectives as the grammar students use to state what they know precisely.

3. Student considerations

"The Myth of Prometheus" (Learn book p. 13) is read aloud, so decoding is not the barrier, but the content includes violence: a human is eaten by a lion, and Prometheus is chained to a cliff while a vulture eats his liver every day. Most fourth graders handle it fine; know your sensitive students and preview it if needed. Students decode Prometheus by syllable (Pro-me-the-us). For the adjective work, students first need to find the nouns. The Teach Book points out that adjectives come after nouns in some languages, so a multilingual student who places an adjective after the noun is showing a transfer pattern, not carelessness. Students who missed lessons in this arc will have less to write about; pair them with a partner and the World Knowledge Chart. The Whip Around asks for a quick one-word adjective, so give think time first.

4. Learning Progress / Instructional Plan (pp. 84-91)

Launch (2 min): choral-read the Content Framing Question and explain the Know lesson. Learn (53 min): Read (15 min), decode Prometheus, read aloud "The Myth of Prometheus," and discuss what students learned about him (challenge: in what ways is it a creation myth, and in what ways a morality myth?); add to the World Knowledge Chart and discuss what Gifts from the Gods taught about myths and their characters. Respond (20 min), adjectives answer how many, what kind, and which one (Sentence Strategies, Learn book p. 83, Strategy 4); students complete the Your Turn sentences, circle nouns on the World Knowledge Chart, and add adjectives; partners orally add adjectives to two more statements; learning task: two knowledge statements about myths that use adjectives (World Knowledge Statements, Learn book p. 2). Observe (18 min), the gallery view of Winged Victory (now in the Louvre), 60 seconds of silent observation, a Whip Around naming one adjective each, then small groups answer "How does the sculpture help you understand victory?" aloud in sentences with adjectives; learning task: two knowledge statements about Winged Victory with adjectives. Land (5 min): Synthesize Learning, revisiting the Essential Question and reinforcing the Knowledge Threads (myths carry universal messages; modern language alludes to Greek and Roman mythology; storytellers use many media; people share stories to entertain, teach, and connect). Rigor comes from synthesizing six lessons and two media, and from precise word choice.

5. Success Criteria / Evidence of learning

- Students will write at least two knowledge statements about myths, each with an adjective (for example, "Achilles was a famous hero who had a weak spot").
- Students will write at least two knowledge statements about Winged Victory that include adjectives.
- Students will say, in a sentence with at least one adjective, how the sculpture helps them understand victory.
- Students will answer the Essential Question with an idea from the arc.

LESSON 8 — Wonder (pp. 94-103)

1. PA Core Standards

- CC.1.3.4.K — Read and comprehend literary fiction on grade level, reading independently and proficiently.
- CC.1.4.4.X — Write routinely over extended time frames (time for research, reflection, and revision) and shorter time frames (a single sitting or a day or two) for a range of discipline-specific tasks, purposes, and audiences.
- CC.1.5.4.A — Engage effectively in a range of collaborative discussions on grade-level topics and texts, building on others’ ideas and expressing their own clearly.

2. Learning Goals / I can statements

- I can notice and wonder about The Invention of Hugo Cabret.
- I can analyze the narrative structure of the Writing Model for Module 2.

Why it matters: Arc B opens a long novel told largely in pictures, and it launches the module's narrative writing. The planner introduced today (setting, characters, problem, events, climax, events, resolution, drawn as a mountain) is the tool students use for Module Task 1 in Lessons 9-16.

3. Student considerations

The Invention of Hugo Cabret tells much of its story through wordless illustrations, which helps students who struggle with decoding but asks every student to read pictures closely, which some students have never been taught to do. The illustrated pages are unpaginated: number them before the lesson (start with "Part One" as page 1; left pages even, right pages odd; white or silver ink shows on the dark pages), or students cannot find pages 4-59. Decide how students will annotate (sticky notes or journals) and model it. The only language support named is lobby ("a large open area inside the entrance of a building"). The writing model ("The Girl, the Goat, and the Fortune") draws on Arc A's myths and Mount Olympus, so it helps to remember that arc. Students who have only written essays (Module 1) may not know story elements by name; the Teach Book reviews them here. A Prologue to Lesson 8 is available.

4. Learning Progress / Instructional Plan (pp. 96-103)

Launch (2 min): introduce the novel and Brian Selznick as author and illustrator; choral-read the Content Framing Question. Learn (53 min): Read (13 min), students explore the covers and pages, then annotate what they notice and wonder as you read the contents page and "A Brief Introduction" aloud and as they examine the illustrations on pp. 4-13; add to the class Notice and Wonder Chart. Respond (20 min), students annotate the illustrations on pp. 14-59 and Mix and Mingle; learning task: four notices and four related questions (Learn book p. 15). Write (20 min), connect narrative to the story Knowledge Card; read aloud the writing model (Learn book pp. 39 and 42; the on-target and advanced versions); review story elements; fill in the class Narrative Writing Planner (Learn book p. 48) for setting, characters, problem and resolution; introduce climax and identify it; learning task: a journal response explaining how the planner helped the writer organize the narrative; discuss why the planner is shaped like a mountain. Land (5 min): knowledge charts. Rigor comes from reading meaning out of images and from reverse-engineering a finished story into its plan.

5. Success Criteria / Evidence of learning

- Students will write four things they notice about the illustrations on pp. 14-59 and four questions connected to those observations.
- Students will identify the setting, characters, problem, climax and resolution of the writing model on the planner.
- Students will write how the planner helped the writer include and order the story elements.

6. Next steps

Lesson 9 has students "follow Hugo" through the text and the illustrations to name the setting, characters and problem on a story map, and it starts Module Task 1. Students begin applying narrative structure in their own writing in Lesson 13. Fluency passage 1 for the novel (Learn book p. 91) starts tonight as follow-up.

LESSON 9 — Organize (pp. 104-115)

1. PA Core Standards

- CC.1.3.4.A — Determine a theme of a text from details in the text; summarize the text.
- CC.1.3.4.B — Cite relevant details from text to support what the text says explicitly and make inferences.
- CC.1.3.4.C — Describe in depth a character, setting, or event in a story or drama, drawing on specific details in the text.
- CC.1.3.4.G — Make connections between the text of a story or drama and a visual or oral presentation of the text, identifying where each version reflects specific descriptions and directions in the text.
- CC.1.3.4.I — Determine or clarify the meaning of unknown and multiple-meaning words and phrases based on grade-level reading and content, choosing flexibly from a range of strategies and tools.
- CC.1.3.4.K — Read and comprehend literary fiction on grade level, reading independently and proficiently.
- CC.1.4.4.M — Write narratives to develop real or imagined experiences or events.
- CC.1.4.4.W — Recall relevant information from experiences or gather relevant information from print and digital sources; take notes and categorize information, and provide a list of sources.
- CC.1.4.4.X — Write routinely over extended time frames (time for research, reflection, and revision) and shorter time frames (a single sitting or a day or two) for a range of discipline-specific tasks, purposes, and audiences.

CC.1.3.4.G (connecting a story to its visual version) fits this lesson closely: students narrate wordless illustrations to understand the plot. CC.1.4.4.M (writing narratives) begins with Module Task 1.

2. Learning Goals / I can statements

- I can organize story elements in The Invention of Hugo Cabret.
- I can explain the words nemesis and victory well enough to understand the prompt for Module Task 1.

Why it matters: Students learn to read the novel's two channels together (text and illustrations), and they meet the Module Task 1 prompt: "Write a narrative in which Hugo experiences victory over a new nemesis in the train station."

3. Student considerations

Students reread "A Brief Introduction" and examine pp. 4-23, then you model narrating the wordless pages (pp. 24-31) before partners narrate pp. 32-45 and read pp. 46-61. Students who have trouble describing what they see need the think-aloud frame (setting, characters, character actions). The pause points while reading the introduction are easy to lose, so the Teach Book suggests writing them into your copy. Module Task 1 depends on nemesis and victory from Arc A; students who missed Lesson 5 need those Knowledge Cards retaught. For the nemesis-and-victor brainstorm, choose character pairs with care: the Teach Book warns that some familiar villain defeats are not appropriate for small-group discussion, and suggests supplying the pairs yourself if needed. The Differentiation Support uses two images (the nemesis causing a problem, then the victory) for students who need a concrete model. A Prologue to Lesson 9 is available.

4. Learning Progress / Instructional Plan (pp. 106-114)

Launch (2 min): choral-read the Content Framing Question; tell students they will "follow Hugo," as the introduction recommends. Learn (53 min): Read (20 min), independent reread through p. 23; read aloud "A Brief Introduction" with pauses to view pp. 4-23; identify the setting (1931, Paris, a train station, morning), who the story is about (Hugo), and who is telling it (Professor H. Alcofrisbas); discuss how Selznick uses text and pictures together; you narrate pp. 24-31 aloud, partners narrate pp. 32-45, students read pp. 46-61; each student writes Hugo's problem on a sticky note (the old man takes his notebook and may burn it). Respond (15 min), learning task: the Characters, Setting and Problem boxes of the Story Map (Learn book p. 16), then a summary of the beginning in the student's own words, compared against your think-aloud summary. Write (18 min), introduce Module Task 1 and its prompt; students fill in the Narrative Writing Planner (Learn book p. 62) for what the prompt already gives them (Hugo and a new nemesis, the train station, Hugo's victory); review nemesis and victory; brainstorm familiar nemesis-and-victor pairs; small groups discuss how each victory happened; learning task: a journal description of one character's victory over a nemesis. Land (5 min): knowledge charts. Rigor comes from narrating images precisely (only what the pictures and text show) and from writing a summary in one's own words.

5. Success Criteria / Evidence of learning

- Students will complete the Characters, Setting and Problem boxes of the Story Map with accurate details (Hugo, the old man, the girl, the Station Inspector; 1931, Paris, the train station and the toy booth; the old man takes Hugo's notebook and threatens to burn it).
- Students will write a summary of the beginning of the book in their own words.
- Students will write how one character achieved a victory over a nemesis, naming the problem the nemesis caused and how it was stopped.

6. Next steps

Lesson 10 continues the story map work with chapters 2-4 and asks students to invent a new nemesis and problem for Hugo, using what they learn about how he lives. Summarizing continues in Lessons 10 and 12; keep the story maps out, since students return to earlier summaries to track how the story develops.

CC.1.4.4.N (orienting the reader with a situation and characters) matches the work of creating a new nemesis and problem for Hugo.

2. Learning Goals / I can statements

- I can describe what is happening in The Invention of Hugo Cabret.
- I can plan how a nemesis contributes to a problem for Module Task 1.

Why it matters: Students track how a story element (Hugo's problem) changes across chapters, and they apply what they learn about Hugo's secret life to design a believable nemesis.

3. Student considerations

Students decode mechanism (mech-an-ism) before reading. You read chapter 2 aloud and narrate pp. 63-80; the Teach Book asks you to narrate only what the text and pictures show (say "a door," not "his secret apartment," which students have not learned about yet) and to use transitions (now, next, then, finally) so students can follow. The clock-part vocabulary on p. 77 (crank, gears, gear shaft, motors) is easier with the illustration on pp. 78-79 in view. Annotation by marginal notes is introduced here; students who write slowly can use short phrases on sticky notes. Partners then read chapters 3-4 (pp. 81-105) on their own, so pair stronger and weaker readers. For the nemesis brainstorm, students need to know how Hugo lives (alone, in secret, stealing to survive, keeping the clocks running); students who did not follow chapter 2 will struggle to invent a nemesis who threatens that life.

4. Learning Progress / Instructional Plan (pp. 118-124)

Launch (2 min). Learn (53 min): Read (18 min), recall Hugo's problem; decode mechanism; read aloud and narrate pp. 63-80; partners reread and jot two or three marginal notes about what Hugo does in the station; partners share an oral summary of chapter 2 and each student writes one; partners read and narrate chapters 3-4, tracking how Hugo tries to get the notebook back. Respond (15 min), independent marginal notes on chapters 3-4; oral summary with a partner; learning task: a written summary of chapters 3-4 in the journal; then compare the summaries to see how Hugo's problem has changed (the old man may burn it, then says he is going home to burn it, then the girl promises he won't). Write (20 min), discuss which existing characters could be Hugo's nemesis and why; reread the prompt and stress that the nemesis must be new; describe how Hugo lives; small groups chart possible nemeses and the problems each would cause (a janitor, a shop owner, a teacher, a train passenger); Gallery Walk; learning task: choose one nemesis and problem and add them to the Narrative Writing Planner (Learn book p. 62). Land (5 min). Rigor comes from tracking change across chapters and from building a character whose threat follows logically from the setting and Hugo's situation.

5. Success Criteria / Evidence of learning

- Students will write a summary of chapters 3-4 with accurate, text-based details from their annotations.
- Students will explain how Hugo's problem has changed from chapter 1 to chapter 4.
- Students will add a new nemesis and a specific problem to the planner that fit Hugo's secret life.

6. Next steps

Lesson 11 rereads the notebook scenes to ask what Hugo's and Papa Georges's reactions reveal, then plans the climax of each student's narrative. Students practice writing about the nemesis and problem in Lesson 13, and summarizing continues with chapter 5 in Lesson 12.

CC.1.3.4.C (describing a character in depth from text details) is the heart of the reading work, and CC.1.4.4.O (using dialogue and description to show characters' responses) anticipates the climax planning.

2. Learning Goals / I can statements

- I can analyze how a character responds to the notebook in The Invention of Hugo Cabret to deepen my understanding of the character.
- I can plan an exciting moment when characters consider a choice for Module Task 1.

Why it matters: The notebook becomes the key object of the novel. Students learn that it comforts Hugo but disturbs Papa Georges, an early clue that the old man knows something about the automaton.

3. Student considerations

Students work with quoted lines (pp. 46, 50 and 51) and reread three scenes (pp. 60-61, 82-83 and 102-103) to annotate at least two details each. That is a lot of rereading, so give partners the page list in writing. Moving from "what he did" to "what it reveals" is the hard step; the Teach Book's prompts (How does Hugo feel about the notebook? What does its worn cover tell you?) help students who stop at retelling. Students who share a home language can discuss Hugo's actions together first. The Papa Georges row is independent work, and students need to notice his shifting expression (fear, then sadness, then anger) on pp. 51-61. Climax is introduced again with its definition; students who missed Lesson 8 need the writing-model planner in front of them. A Prologue to Lesson 11 is available.

4. Learning Progress / Instructional Plan (pp. 130-137)

Launch (2 min). Learn (53 min): Read (20 min), recall Hugo's problem; read aloud pp. 46-51 while students annotate how Hugo treats the notebook; display the three quotations and think aloud; model the Actions column on Characters' Actions (Learn book p. 17); partners discuss what the actions reveal (he is attached to it, it comforts him); reread the three scenes where the old man takes the notebook, annotate, list at least three actions (he demands it back, accuses the old man, follows him home, wants to tackle him), and write what they reveal. Respond (15 min), partners reread pp. 51-61 and 82-83 for Papa Georges's reactions; learning task: independently complete the third row with his actions and what they reveal; discuss the contrast (comfort for Hugo, discomfort for the old man) and predict whether he will burn it. Write (18 min), introduce climax; think aloud to turn the writing model's problem into a choice ("Will the soldier or the girl retrieve the helmet?"); students rewrite their own problem as a choice question and add it next to the Climax box; partners discuss how to make that moment exciting; learning task: describe the climax in the planner. Land (5 min). Rigor comes from inference: students must go beyond the text to explain motive, and from turning a problem into a dilemma that creates tension.

5. Success Criteria / Evidence of learning

- Students will record at least three of Hugo's actions with the notebook and explain what they reveal about him.
- Students will record Papa Georges's reactions and explain that they suggest he recognizes the automaton drawings.
- Students will write a climax in the planner that turns their story's problem into a choice and adds excitement.

6. Next steps

Lesson 12 goes back in time to Hugo's father and the automaton, which explains why the notebook matters so much. Students practice explaining a character's reactions again in Lesson 13 (Tableaus of Hugo's reactions in chapters 6-7) and write their climaxes into a draft starting in Lesson 13.

2. Learning Goals / I can statements

- I can summarize chapter 5 of The Invention of Hugo Cabret.
- I can plan the events of a narrative for Module Task 1.

Why it matters: Chapter 5 is the backstory that makes the whole novel make sense: Hugo's father, the automaton, and the fire. Students also learn that a story needs events between the problem and the climax and between the climax and the resolution.

3. Student considerations

The Teach Book warns that this chapter describes the death of Hugo's father in a fire, and that some students may have personal experiences that make it hard to discuss. Know your students, consider a private heads-up, and keep the discussion on the story's events. The chapter moves back in time and then returns to the present ("Three months had passed since then"), so make the time shift explicit for students who lose track of sequence. Vocabulary: acrobat, frayed and trapeze (define with synonyms) and automaton (au-tom-a-ton), taught through the root auto, "self," and inferred from the illustration on pp. 110-111; the root returns in Lesson 36. Students who struggle with the summary can use the Teach Book's two-step support: say aloud what happened up to the fire, write it, then do the same for after the fire. Students read pp. 135-143 (chapter 6) as homework before Lesson 13, so plan for students who will not.

4. Learning Progress / Instructional Plan (pp. 142-148)

Launch (2 min). Learn (53 min): Read (20 min), students ask questions about Hugo's father; read aloud pp. 107-114 and narrate the illustrations; annotate details of Hugo's past; decode automaton, learn the root auto and infer the meaning; continue reading aloud through p. 121 and silently narrate pp. 122-123; annotate pp. 117-123; discuss the tragic event (his father was trapped in a fire at the museum); Think-Pair-Share a two- or three-sentence account of the past; students read pp. 124-132 on their own and annotate what happened after the fire. Respond (18 min), partners draft sentences about after the fire (Uncle Claude, the disappearance, finding the automaton); learning task: a journal summary of Hugo's past; read pp. 132-133 and discuss what Hugo believes the automaton's note will do. Write (15 min), the planner's two Events boxes; find the events before and after the climax in the writing model; partners tell their own events aloud and check "Am I able to visualize what is happening?"; learning task: at least two events before the climax and two after it in the planner. Land (5 min): knowledge charts and the chapter 6 reading assignment. Rigor comes from summarizing a nonlinear chapter accurately and from planning a logical cause-and-effect sequence.

5. Success Criteria / Evidence of learning

- Students will write a summary of Hugo's past that covers events up to and including the fire and events after it.
- Students will explain what an automaton is and connect auto to "self."
- Students will add at least two events leading to the climax and two leading to the resolution in their planners.

LESSON 21 — Reading Comprehension Assessment 1 (pp. 240-243)

1. PA Core Standards

None assigned. Great Minds' PA alignment guide does not code Lesson 21 to a standard; it is an assessment lesson.

2. Learning Goals / I can statements

- I can show what I know about myths and stories and use reading comprehension skills with a new text related to myths.

Why it matters: This is the module's first reading comprehension assessment. Its results decide what Lesson 22 reteaches.

3. Student considerations

The assessment has four sections: an oral fluency reading of an excerpt from a new text related to myths, questions on important module words and concepts, comprehension questions on the new text, and a self-reflection on confidence and challenges. The fluency section is read aloud individually, so plan how you will listen to each student while the others work. Apply each student's testing accommodations as the Assessment Guide and your school allow. Students can take it on paper or on the digital platform; the platform gives practice with technology-enhanced items. Students who finish early read a volume of reading book, and students who need more time can use that block.

4. Learning Progress / Instructional Plan (pp. 242-243)

Launch (2 min): choral-read the Essential Question and explain that the assessment text adds to students' knowledge. Learn (55 min): Respond (40 min), introduce the four sections and administer Reading Comprehension Assessment 1 as the Assessment Guide directs; Read (15 min), volume of reading for students who have finished. Land (3 min): partners share one thing they learned about myths from the assessment text.

5. Success Criteria / Evidence of learning

- Students will complete all four sections of Reading Comprehension Assessment 1.
- Scoring follows the Assessment Guide. Use the results to choose the questions and the module terms to review in Lesson 22.

6. Next steps

Before Lesson 22, use the Assessment Guide and the class results to choose which questions to revisit and which module terms to review. Students who struggled with the fluency section are the ones to watch during fluency passage 3.

LESSON 22 — Responsive Teaching (pp. 244-249)

1. PA Core Standards

- CC.1.1.4.D — Know and apply grade-level phonics and word analysis skills in decoding words.
- CC.1.2.4.B — Refer to details and examples in text to support what the text says explicitly and make inferences.
- CC.1.2.4.J — Acquire and use accurately grade-appropriate conversational, general academic, and domain-specific words and phrases, including those that signal precise actions, emotions, or states of being and that are basic to a particular topic.
- CC.1.2.4.K — Determine or clarify the meaning of unknown and multiple-meaning words and phrases based on grade-level reading and content, choosing flexibly from a range of strategies and tools.
- CC.1.2.4.L — Read and comprehend literary nonfiction and informational text on grade level, reading independently and proficiently.

CC.1.2.4.K (strategies for unknown words) matches the root work with lum and lumin.

2. Learning Goals / I can statements

- I can analyze relevant questions on Reading Comprehension Assessment 1.
- I can explain how to identify the correct answers.

Why it matters: Students learn from their own assessment, hearing the text read fluently and talking through how to find correct answers, and they deepen word knowledge with roots and module terms.

3. Student considerations

The questions you review should come from your class data, so the lesson looks different in each room. Some students are anxious about reviewing items they missed; keep the focus on the reasoning, not on who got it wrong. For students who cannot yet explain how they found an answer, think aloud first, then have partners practice. The root work returns to illuminate from Lesson 6 (lum, lumin, "light, glow") on the Word Parts Web (Learn book p. 20); multilingual students can look for cognates in their home languages (for example, Spanish luz and luminoso). The vocabulary activity uses Knowledge Cards that you choose: endure, fortune, illuminate, myth, nemesis, story and victory.

4. Learning Progress / Instructional Plan (pp. 246-248)

Launch (2 min): choral-read the Essential Question. Learn (55 min): Read (10 min), read the assessment text aloud, modeling fluent reading. Respond (25 min), learning task: display the questions you selected and discuss how to identify each correct answer, following the Assessment Guide's responsive teaching notes. Engage (20 min), find illuminate in the Glossary (Learn book p. 112) and name its root; partners brainstorm lum and lumin words on the Word Parts Web (such as illumination, luminous, luminary); students sketch two or three of the words and discuss how the drawings relate to "light"; then one Knowledge Card activity, either Share What You Know (a knowledge statement using the term) or Create a Web. Land (3 min): partners share one thing they learned from working through the assessment.

5. Success Criteria / Evidence of learning

- Students will explain how to find the correct answer for at least one reviewed question, pointing to evidence in the text.
- Students will add at least three lum or lumin words to the Word Parts Web and explain how each relates to light.
- Students will use a module term correctly in a knowledge statement or a word web.

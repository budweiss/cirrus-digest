LESSON 23 — Organize (pp. 250-259)

1. PA Core Standards

- CC.1.3.4.A — Determine a theme of a text from details in the text; summarize the text.
- CC.1.3.4.B — Cite relevant details from text to support what the text says explicitly and make inferences.
- CC.1.3.4.C — Describe in depth a character, setting, or event in a story or drama, drawing on specific details in the text.
- CC.1.3.4.I — Determine or clarify the meaning of unknown and multiple-meaning words and phrases based on grade-level reading and content, choosing flexibly from a range of strategies and tools.
- CC.1.3.4.J — Acquire and use accurately grade-appropriate conversational, general academic, and domain-specific words and phrases, including those that signal precise actions, emotions, or states of being and that are basic to a particular topic.
- CC.1.3.4.K — Read and comprehend literary fiction on grade level, reading independently and proficiently.
- CC.1.4.4.M — Write narratives to develop real or imagined experiences or events.
- CC.1.4.4.Q — Choose words and phrases to convey ideas precisely.
- CC.1.4.4.T — With guidance and support from peers and adults, develop and strengthen writing as needed by planning, revising, and editing.
- CC.1.4.4.W — Recall relevant information from experiences or gather relevant information from print and digital sources; take notes and categorize information, and provide a list of sources.
- CC.1.4.4.X — Write routinely over extended time frames (time for research, reflection, and revision) and shorter time frames (a single sitting or a day or two) for a range of discipline-specific tasks, purposes, and audiences.
- CC.1.5.4.A — Engage effectively in a range of collaborative discussions on grade-level topics and texts, building on others’ ideas and expressing their own clearly.
- CC.1.5.4.B — Paraphrase portions of a text read aloud or information presented in diverse media and formats, including visually, quantitatively, and orally.

2. Learning Goals / I can statements

- I can summarize part 2, chapters 1-2 of The Invention of Hugo Cabret.
- I can use descriptive language to strengthen a narrative for Module Task 2.

Why it matters: Part 2 opens a new mystery, the link between Papa Georges and the automaton, and the box of drawings reveals who he once was. Students also start revising their own narratives for vivid language.

3. Student considerations

Students start part 2 on their own (p. 251 and the illustration on pp. 252-253), then you read aloud pp. 259-271. The chapter has conflict that some students find upsetting: Isabelle and Hugo fight, the drawing rips, and Hugo's fingers are slammed in a door. Show a picture of an armoire (a wardrobe or large cabinet), since few students will know the word. Students read pp. 272-297 on their own, much of it illustrations of Papa Georges's drawings, so remind them to read the pictures closely. Papa Georges's reaction (ripping the drawings, crying) can be hard to interpret; the Mix and Mingle on why he responds that way lets students try out ideas. For the revision, students who struggle to find places to add detail can do a quick peer review first (where is the scene hard to picture?) or circle nouns and verbs and make one more specific. A Prologue to Lesson 23 is available.

4. Learning Progress / Instructional Plan (pp. 252-258)

Launch (2 min): choral-read the Content Framing Question and preview the new mystery. Learn (53 min): Read (20 min), students read p. 251 and view pp. 252-253, then discuss what the automaton drew and what questions Hugo has; read aloud pp. 259-262 and name the new mystery (the connection between Papa Georges and the automaton); continue reading aloud through p. 268, discussing what Hugo and Isabelle do (Hugo recognizes a scene from his father's favorite movie, Isabelle accuses him of stealing the automaton, the drawing rips, she goes to ask Mama Jeanne); read aloud pp. 269-271 (the armoire and the hidden panel); students read pp. 272-297. Respond (13 min), discuss what they find (a box of hundreds of drawings by Georges Méliès); look closely at the drawings on pp. 284-297; students read pp. 298-299 and annotate Papa Georges's response; learning task: a journal summary of how Papa Georges responds to his drawings; Mix and Mingle on why he responds that way. Write (20 min), find the precise language and sensory details in the quotation from p. 264 (crushing, fingers, crunch, screamed) and discuss how they help the reader picture the scene; students mark at least three places in their own narrative to add detail, brainstorm words, and practice one revision orally with a partner; learning task: add precise language and sensory details to Module Task 1; share in writing groups. Land (5 min). Rigor comes from summarizing a character's reaction precisely and from revising for effect, not just length.

5. Success Criteria / Evidence of learning

- Students will write a summary of how Papa Georges responds to the drawings (he whispers, asks who made them, rips them, cries, and Mama Jeanne helps him to bed).
- Students will name the precise language and sensory details in a quoted passage and explain their effect.
- Students will add precise language and sensory details in at least three places in their narrative.

6. Next steps

Lesson 24 compares the same scenes in the screenplay Hugo: The Shooting Script and teaches the order of adjectives for the next revision. Students summarize again in Lesson 26 and use descriptive language in the End-of-Module Task narrative in Lesson 33. Fluency passage 3 finishes with a small-group performance after Lesson 24.

- CC.1.4.4.O — Use dialogue and descriptions to develop experiences and events or show the responses of characters to situations; use concrete words and phrases and sensory details to convey experiences and events precisely.
- CC.1.4.4.R — Demonstrate a grade-appropriate command of the conventions of standard English grammar, usage, capitalization, punctuation, and spelling.

CC.1.3.4.E (differences between drama and prose) and CC.1.3.4.G (a story and its visual or oral version) both fit the screenplay comparison.

2. Learning Goals / I can statements

- I can identify similarities and differences between texts.
- I can use and order adjectives appropriately for Module Task 2.

Why it matters: Students meet a new form, the screenplay, and see the same story told for actors and filmmakers instead of for readers. The adjective-order rule gives them a concrete revision tool.

3. Student considerations

The screenplay excerpt (Learn book p. 21) looks unlike anything students have read: centered character names in capitals, no quotation marks, scene headings (INT. means inside, EXT. means outside), and parenthetical directions. Walk through the format line by line, as the Teach Book does, before students read on their own. The excerpt includes hard words (circumlocutions, gentility, impoverished), so define them with synonyms. Performing the scenes (groups of three for scene 56 and four for scene 57) motivates most students; set safety rules (no standing on chairs, no pretend falls), and let students highlight their own part in one color for dialogue and another for actions. Adjective order is intuitive for many native English speakers but not for multilingual learners; the Order of Adjectives Chart (Learn book p. 30) makes the rule visible. A Prologue to Lesson 24 is available.

4. Learning Progress / Instructional Plan (pp. 262-268)

Launch (2 min): display the screenplay and choral-read the Content Framing Question. Learn (53 min): Read (18 min), notice and wonder about the screenplay; introduce screenplay (Knowledge Card); compare its format with the novel (centered text, capitals, underlining, no quotation marks, directions to actors); read it aloud; explain scene headings and find the setting of scene 56 (inside the apartment, in the evening); read scene 56, lines 5-22, and use the formatting to answer what is happening, who is speaking, what they say, and what they do. Respond (20 min), groups rehearse scene 56 or 57 and then perform for a group with the other scene; learning task: a journal response on how the screenplay is similar to and different from the novel. Write (15 min), review how adjectives describe nouns; introduce the Order of Adjectives Chart (categories such as quantity, opinion, size, shape, condition and age) and add examples; partners describe Hugo's notebook with several adjectives in the right order (for example, "small square cardboard"); learning task: add two or more correctly ordered adjectives to at least two nouns in Module Task 1. Land (5 min). Rigor comes from comparing how two forms tell one story and from applying a grammar rule in the student's own writing.

5. Success Criteria / Evidence of learning

- Students will identify at least two screenplay formatting features and what each tells the actors or filmmakers.
- Students will write at least one similarity and one difference between the screenplay and the novel (for example, the novel shows Papa Georges's drawings as illustrations, while the screenplay describes them in words).
- Students will revise at least two nouns in their narrative with two or more adjectives in the correct order.

6. Next steps

Lesson 25 compares the novel and the screenplay again, this time asking why each writer chose different techniques for their audience, and it teaches punctuation for effect in dialogue. Students order adjectives in the End-of-Module Task narrative in Lesson 33.

CC.1.3.4.E and CC.1.3.4.G continue from Lesson 24: the lesson compares how prose and a screenplay present the same scene.

2. Learning Goals / I can statements

- I can examine writers' craft in Hugo: The Shooting Script and The Invention of Hugo Cabret.
- I can explain how writers use punctuation for effect.

Why it matters: Students learn that writers make choices for an audience. Selznick writes and draws for young readers, and Logan writes for actors and filmmakers. That idea of audience feeds directly into their own narratives.

3. Student considerations

The idea of audience is abstract; the Teach Book's question "Who reads and uses a screenplay?" helps students who cannot answer "Who is the audience?" Students narrate pp. 272-281 with a partner, then read scene 57 lines 57-79 and annotate how Logan turns pictures into words (action lines, precise words such as "tiptoes," ellipses, capital letters such as "SNAPS"). Comparing two texts side by side is demanding for students who read slowly, so consider assigning partners one text each. For punctuation for effect, reading the quotations aloud with expression helps students hear the difference an exclamation point makes; students who struggle to name the emotion can draw an emoji for it first. Students read part 2, chapter 3 as homework before Lesson 26, focusing on Hugo's plan.

4. Learning Progress / Instructional Plan (pp. 272-278)

Launch (2 min): display both texts and choral-read the Content Framing Question. Learn (53 min): Read (18 min), discuss Selznick's audience (young readers) and how he holds their attention (descriptive words, detailed illustrations, suspense, interesting characters); partners narrate pp. 272-281; discuss the audience for a screenplay (actors, directors, filmmakers, moviegoers); students read scene 57 lines 57-79 and annotate Logan's techniques. Respond (15 min), students annotate p. 282 and the illustrations on pp. 284-297, then scene 57 lines 80-109, and compare the techniques (for example, Selznick's literal "scattered across the floor" against Logan's figurative "cyclone of papers"); learning task: a journal response to "Why do screenwriters and book authors use different techniques to tell a similar story?" Write (20 min), read three quotations from p. 262 aloud with expression and discuss what the punctuation shows about each character's emotion; students add punctuation for effect to dialogue in their own narrative and explain the emotion to a partner (challenge: ellipses, dashes or capitals); learning task: a journal explanation of how writers use punctuation in dialogue to show emotion. Land (5 min): knowledge charts and the chapter 3 assignment. Rigor comes from explaining why a writer chose a technique, not only naming it.

5. Success Criteria / Evidence of learning

- Students will explain that book authors and screenwriters use different techniques because they write for different audiences.
- Students will compare at least one technique each writer uses for the same scene.
- Students will add punctuation for effect to their dialogue and explain the emotion it shows.

6. Next steps

Students analyze writers' craft again in Module 3 and use punctuation for effect in the End-of-Module Task in Lesson 33. Fluency passage 4 (Learn book p. 97) starts tonight.

2. Learning Goals / I can statements

- I can summarize what is happening in part 2, chapter 4 of The Invention of Hugo Cabret.
- I can revise a narrative for Module Task 2 using the checklist and peer feedback.

Why it matters: Chapter 4 ("The Invention of Dreams") reveals who Papa Georges really is, the filmmaker Georges Méliès, and students finish and polish Module Task 2.

3. Student considerations

The lesson depends on the chapter 3 homework; students who did not read it can catch up during the group sequencing of Hugo's plan (the Teach Book points to p. 303 as the place to start). Chapter 4 is read with a partner and packs in historical facts (A Train Arrives in the Station, made in 1895; A Trip to the Moon, by Georges Méliès), so students may mix up the films; a quick class list helps. Vocabulary: invention, with two meanings (a useful new device or process; the act of creating something new); the discussion of which meaning fits the chapter title stretches stronger students. The Mix and Mingle needs each student to write one fact on a sticky note first, so students who need language support should rehearse orally before writing. For peer review, decide the pairings and how students will see each other's writing and checklist ahead of time; students who marked revisions on paper need time to produce a clean draft before they review.

4. Learning Progress / Instructional Plan (pp. 282-286)

Launch (2 min). Learn (53 min): Read (19 min), small groups sequence four actions in Hugo's plan from chapter 3 (taking the toy booth keys, searching the booth, asking at the bookstore about the first movies, getting directions to the Film Academy library from Monsieur Labisse); introduce invention and read the chapter 4 title; partners read chapter 4 and annotate what Hugo learns at the library. Respond (13 min), students reread pp. 354-360 and write one fact about Papa Georges on a sticky note; Mix and Mingle (he was a magician, then one of the first filmmakers to make movies that did not show real life, used tricks to make things appear and disappear, made A Trip to the Moon, and people believe he is dead); learning task: a journal summary of what Hugo learns about Georges Méliès, using the word invention; discuss which definition of invention fits "The Invention of Dreams." Write (21 min), students finish a clean draft if needed; review the Writing and Language rows of the Checklist for Module Task 2 (Learn book p. 69); students check their own narrative (Learn book p. 65); partners in writing groups exchange narratives and write sticky-note feedback (one thing they like, one thing that confuses them, one suggestion); learning task: revise using the checklist and the feedback. Land (5 min). Rigor comes from synthesizing facts into a summary that uses new vocabulary, and from acting on peer feedback.

5. Success Criteria / Evidence of learning

- Students will write a summary of what Hugo learns about Georges Méliès, with specific details and the word invention.
- Students will give a partner three pieces of feedback (a strength, a point of confusion, a suggestion).
- Students will revise their narrative to meet the Module Task 2 checklist.

2. Learning Goals / I can statements

- I can summarize part 2, chapters 5-6 of The Invention of Hugo Cabret.
- I can create a drawing to enhance a narrative.

Why it matters: Hugo and Isabelle move from rivals to friends who are honest with each other and work together. Students also apply what they learned from Selznick's illustrations by illustrating their own story.

3. Student considerations

Half the class follows Isabelle and half follows Hugo while reading chapter 5, then partners swap what their character shared. This works well, but students must stick to their character, so give each half a different sticky-note color. Chapter 6 is read independently; two colors of sticky notes (one per character) help students track who does what. Chapter 5 includes Papa Georges's illness and the family's money worries (no money for medicine because the booth is closed), which may resonate with some students. The drawing task is a strength for many students who struggle with writing; reassure reluctant artists that the drawing's job is to help a reader picture the story, not to be perfect. Save the drawings for Lesson 28.

4. Learning Progress / Instructional Plan (pp. 292-297)

Launch (2 min). Learn (53 min): Read (18 min), Think-Pair-Share on whether Hugo and Isabelle are friends at this point (evidence on both sides); students read chapter 5 focused on their assigned character and annotate what that character shares; partners from the two groups swap (Isabelle shares Papa Georges's illness and the family's money trouble; Hugo shares that Papa Georges made movies, his visit to the library, his invitation to Etienne and René Tabard, and his own life story); predict Hugo's idea from p. 368; students read chapter 6 on their own. Respond (10 min), discuss what they do in chapter 6 (run the toy booth, read and discuss myths, talk about machines and Papa Georges, climb the clock tower); learning task: a journal summary of chapters 5-6; discuss how the relationship has changed (frame: "At the beginning of part 2, Hugo and Isabelle..., but now they..."). Write (25 min), discuss how Selznick's illustrations add to the story (they show characters and settings, add action, control pace, show feelings); students list two or three drawing ideas that would help a reader of their narrative, choose one with a partner, and then complete the learning task: create a drawing for the narrative (challenge: a sequence of drawings in Selznick's style). Land (5 min). Rigor comes from piecing together a full picture from two perspectives and from choosing an image that adds meaning to the writing.

5. Success Criteria / Evidence of learning

- Students will write a summary of chapters 5-6 with specific details from both characters' perspectives.
- Students will explain, with evidence, how Hugo and Isabelle's relationship has changed.
- Students will create a drawing that helps a reader understand or picture part of their narrative.

2. Learning Goals / I can statements

- I can examine the use of figurative language in The Invention of Hugo Cabret to better understand a character.
- I can share an original narrative and drawing.

Why it matters: The chapter "Purpose" states the novel's big idea: if the world is a machine, every person, like every part, is there for a reason. Students then celebrate their finished narratives with an audience.

3. Student considerations

Vocabulary: purpose ("the aim or goal of a person; what a person is trying to do or become"). The figurative comparisons are layered (broken machines compared to people without a purpose, Papa Georges compared to a broken machine, the world compared to a big machine), so take them one at a time, as the Teach Book does. The opinion line-up ("Do you agree that everyone has a purpose?") is physical and public; students who do not want to commit in front of others can share their reasons with a partner instead. Some students may find questions about purpose personal, so keep it about the story's idea. For the showcase, mix English proficiency levels in groups so multilingual learners hear strong models, and consider new groups for a new audience. Students who are anxious about reading aloud can practice first or record audio, which the Teach Book allows. A Prologue to Lesson 28 is available.

4. Learning Progress / Instructional Plan (pp. 302-307)

Launch (2 min). Learn (53 min): Read (17 min), introduce purpose; recall Hugo's purpose in part 1 (fixing the automaton); read aloud p. 374 and discuss how Hugo explains the purpose of machines; continue reading through p. 375 and identify the comparisons (broken machines and people without a purpose; Papa Georges and a broken machine); Repeated Reading of p. 378 and identify the comparison of the world to a big machine, with Hugo and Isabelle as its parts. Respond (13 min), learning task: a journal response on what Hugo's comparisons show about his understanding of the world and his purpose in it, using the word purpose; opinion line-up and partner discussion on whether everyone has a purpose. Write (23 min), learning task: in small groups, students read their narratives aloud fluently and share their drawings (or play audio recordings); a journal reflection: what they learned about writing a narrative, one thing they did well, one thing to improve next time. Land (5 min). Rigor comes from interpreting an extended metaphor and from reflecting honestly on one's own writing process.

5. Success Criteria / Evidence of learning

- Students will explain how Hugo's comparisons show his belief that everyone has a purpose, citing the figurative language.
- Students will give and defend an opinion on whether everyone has a purpose.
- Students will read their narrative fluently, share their drawing, and write a reflection naming a strength and a goal.

The reflection goals students set today are worth keeping visible during that task. Students examine figurative language again in Module 3 and present an End-of-Module Task narrative in Lesson 39.

Keep the focus on reasoning, not on who missed what; think aloud first for students who cannot yet explain how they found an answer. The root auto returns from Lesson 12 (automaton); the Word Parts Web (Learn book p. 32) invites words such as autobiography, autograph, automatic, automobile and autopilot. Multilingual students can look for cognates (for example, Spanish automóvil and autógrafo). Sketching the words supports students who learn visually. The vocabulary activity can use any Module 2 Knowledge Card, so choose terms that support the Knowledge Threads you want to strengthen before the Finale.

4. Learning Progress / Instructional Plan (pp. 380-382)

Launch (2 min): choral-read the Essential Question. Learn (55 min): Read (10 min), read the assessment text aloud, modeling fluent reading. Respond (25 min), learning task: display the questions you selected and discuss how to identify each correct answer, following the Assessment Guide. Engage (20 min), find automaton in the Glossary (Learn book p. 112) and name its root; partners add auto words to the Word Parts Web, sketch two or three, and discuss how the drawings relate to "self"; then one Knowledge Card activity (Share What You Know or Create a Web). Land (3 min): partners share one thing they learned from working through the assessment.

5. Success Criteria / Evidence of learning

- Students will explain how to find the correct answer for at least one reviewed question, using evidence from the text.
- Students will add at least three auto words to the Word Parts Web and explain how each relates to "self."
- Students will use a module term correctly in a knowledge statement or a word web.

6. Next steps

The Module Finale (Lessons 37-40) completes, records, and celebrates the End-of-Module Task. Students discuss assessment responses again in Module 3.

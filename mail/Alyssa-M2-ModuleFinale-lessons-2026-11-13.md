LESSON 37 — Module Finale, Know (pp. 384-391)

1. PA Core Standards

- CC.1.3.4.A — Determine a theme of a text from details in the text; summarize the text.
- CC.1.3.4.B — Cite relevant details from text to support what the text says explicitly and make inferences.
- CC.1.3.4.I — Determine or clarify the meaning of unknown and multiple-meaning words and phrases based on grade-level reading and content, choosing flexibly from a range of strategies and tools.
- CC.1.3.4.K — Read and comprehend literary fiction on grade level, reading independently and proficiently.
- CC.1.4.4.M — Write narratives to develop real or imagined experiences or events.
- CC.1.4.4.N — Orient the reader by establishing a situation and introducing a narrator and/or characters.
- CC.1.4.4.O — Use dialogue and descriptions to develop experiences and events or show the responses of characters to situations; use concrete words and phrases and sensory details to convey experiences and events precisely.
- CC.1.4.4.P — Organize an event sequence that unfolds naturally, using a variety of transitional words and phrases to manage the sequence of events; provide a conclusion that follows from the narrated experiences and events.
- CC.1.4.4.Q — Choose words and phrases to convey ideas precisely.
- CC.1.4.4.R — Demonstrate a grade-appropriate command of the conventions of standard English grammar, usage, capitalization, punctuation, and spelling.
- CC.1.4.4.X — Write routinely over extended time frames (time for research, reflection, and revision) and shorter time frames (a single sitting or a day or two) for a range of discipline-specific tasks, purposes, and audiences.
- CC.1.5.4.A — Engage effectively in a range of collaborative discussions on grade-level topics and texts, building on others’ ideas and expressing their own clearly.
- CC.1.5.4.D — Report on a topic or text, tell a story, or recount an experience in an organized manner, using appropriate facts and relevant, descriptive details to support main ideas or themes; speaking clearly with adequate volume, appropriate pacing, and clear pronunciation.
- CC.1.5.4.E — Differentiate between contexts that require formal English versus informal situations.

CC.1.5.4.A (collaborative discussion) carries the Socratic seminar, and the narrative writing codes (CC.1.4.4.M-R) cover finishing the End-of-Module Task draft.

2. Learning Goals / I can statements

- I can apply what I know about what makes a story endure.
- I can draft a narrative using a planner and checklist for the End-of-Module Task.

Why it matters: The finale brings the module back to its first word, endure. Students build criteria for a story that lasts from the myths, then argue in a Socratic seminar whether The Invention of Hugo Cabret is one.

3. Student considerations

Small groups write "Five Requirements for a Story to Endure," and students who need support can use the excerpts from Understanding Greek Myths and the What Are Myths? list from Lesson 2. The Socratic seminar uses all three of the module's speaking and listening goals (ask questions to deepen understanding; review and build on others' ideas; elaborate on evidence); have students annotate the Talking Tool frames they plan to use, and use the Speaking and Listening Goal Tracker to focus on students who have not yet met the goals. Writing a position before the seminar gives quieter students something to say. Students arrive at different stages of the End-of-Module Task draft, so help those who are behind decide which remaining element to write first.

4. Learning Progress / Instructional Plan (pp. 386-390)

Launch (2 min): explain the finale and choral-read the Essential Question. Learn (53 min): Read (15 min), revisit Gifts from the Gods and the endure Knowledge Card; groups create Five Requirements for a Story to Endure (for example, it entertains, teaches life lessons, explains how things came to be, has relatable characters and problems, stays relevant); students write an evidence-based answer to "Is The Invention of Hugo Cabret an enduring story?" Respond (20 min), review the three speaking and listening goals and the Talking Tool; Socratic seminar on the question, with extension questions (Why would one entertaining story endure and not another? Can we predict which stories will endure?); learning task: add to or revise the journal response based on the discussion. Write (18 min), partners tell the beginning, middle and end of their narratives; students mark the Checklist for the End-of-Module Task (Learn book p. 75) for what is already in the draft; learning task: complete the draft by adding the remaining elements from the planner and checklist; journal: one part they want people to notice and one part that was hard to write. Land (5 min): Synthesize Learning (what students learned from discussing which stories endure). Rigor comes from building criteria and applying them to a new text, and from revising one's own thinking after hearing others.

5. Success Criteria / Evidence of learning

- Students will write and support a position on whether The Invention of Hugo Cabret is an enduring story, using the class criteria.
- Students will contribute to the Socratic seminar using at least one of the module's speaking and listening goals.
- Students will revise their written response based on the discussion.
- Students will complete a full End-of-Module Task draft that includes every planner and checklist element.

6. Next steps

Lesson 38 uses the checklist for peer feedback and has students record a fluent audio reading of their narrative. Socratic seminars return at the end of every module this year, so note who needs more support with the speaking and listening goals before Module 3.

LESSON 38 — Module Finale, Know (pp. 392-399)

1. PA Core Standards

- CC.1.1.4.E — Read with accuracy and fluency to support comprehension.
- CC.1.4.4.M — Write narratives to develop real or imagined experiences or events.
- CC.1.4.4.Q — Choose words and phrases to convey ideas precisely.
- CC.1.4.4.R — Demonstrate a grade-appropriate command of the conventions of standard English grammar, usage, capitalization, punctuation, and spelling.
- CC.1.4.4.T — With guidance and support from peers and adults, develop and strengthen writing as needed by planning, revising, and editing.
- CC.1.4.4.U — With some guidance and support, use technology, including the Internet, to produce and publish writing as well as to interact and collaborate with others; demonstrate sufficient command of keyboarding skills to type a minimum of one page in a single sitting.
- CC.1.4.4.X — Write routinely over extended time frames (time for research, reflection, and revision) and shorter time frames (a single sitting or a day or two) for a range of discipline-specific tasks, purposes, and audiences.
- CC.1.5.4.F — Add audio recordings and visual displays to presentations when appropriate to enhance the development of main ideas or themes.

CC.1.4.4.U (using technology to produce and publish writing) and CC.1.1.4.E (fluency) match the audio recording of the narrative.

2. Learning Goals / I can statements

- I can give a peer feedback that supports their revision for the End-of-Module Task.
- I can read a narrative aloud fluently for the End-of-Module Task.

Why it matters: Students practice the full writing process (specific, checklist-based feedback, revision, then publishing) and bring the module's fluency work into a real product, an audio recording of their own story.

3. Student considerations

Students will be at different stages, so the Teach Book suggests adjusting the timing for students who still need to finish their drafts. Feedback must be specific to a checklist criterion; model one example (for instance, "I'd like to know what this character looks like") and invite a positive example too. Decide peer pairings and how partners will see each other's writing (the Learn book page or a shared digital document). For the recording, plan the devices and a quiet place; some students are uncomfortable hearing their own voice, so let them whisper-rehearse first and remind everyone that self-correcting is part of fluent reading. Students who struggle with fluency can record in short sections.

4. Learning Progress / Instructional Plan (pp. 394-397)

Launch (2 min): display all module texts and choral-read the Essential Question. Learn (53 min): Write (30 min), discuss how the checklist can guide feedback (what is missing, what is already strong); think aloud to model specific feedback; learning task: partners exchange narratives, give feedback using the Checklist for the End-of-Module Task (Learn book p. 75), discuss it, and revise. Write (23 min), find the remaining checklist criteria (record the entire story; read fluently); review the Fluency Reference Chart and how each element makes a recording entertaining; students whisper-read their narratives, self-correcting as they go; learning task: record a fluent reading of the narrative. Land (5 min): Synthesize Learning with the ELA Knowledge Chart (what students learned about English language arts and how reviewing someone else's writing helped them grow). Rigor comes from evaluating writing against criteria and from applying all four fluency elements to a published reading.

5. Success Criteria / Evidence of learning

- Students will give their partner specific feedback on at least one checklist element, plus one strength.
- Students will revise their narrative using peer feedback and the checklist.
- Students will record their entire narrative, reading with accuracy, phrasing, expression, and an appropriate rate.

6. Next steps

After the End-of-Module Task, use the Assessment Guide for next steps on feedback and future writing instruction. Lesson 39 adds a drawing to the narrative and shares both with writing groups. Students practice peer feedback again in Module 3.

LESSON 39 — Module Finale, Know (pp. 400-405)

1. PA Core Standards

- CC.1.5.4.F — Add audio recordings and visual displays to presentations when appropriate to enhance the development of main ideas or themes.

2. Learning Goals / I can statements

- I can create a drawing that enhances a narrative.

Why it matters: Students use what they learned from Selznick's illustrations to add a visual that gives details or builds suspense, then share their finished work with their writing group.

3. Student considerations

The whole learning block is one task, so students who finish early can add detail or help a partner. Students who are unsure what to draw can choose between adding details about a character, setting or event and building excitement or suspense, and can look at model illustrations (p. 63 of Gifts from the Gods and pp. 448-451 of The Invention of Hugo Cabret). Reassure reluctant artists that the goal is to help the reader, not to show artistic skill. Reading aloud to the writing group again uses the fluency practiced in Lesson 38.

4. Learning Progress / Instructional Plan (pp. 402-404)

Launch (2 min): display all module texts and choral-read the Essential Question. Learn (53 min): Write (53 min), discuss the ways an illustration can enhance a story (it shows characters, settings and details; shows actions and feelings; adds events not in the text; builds excitement or suspense) and list the ideas; students choose one place in their narrative to enhance and sketch or jot ideas; learning task: create a drawing that adds details or creates suspense or excitement; writing groups take turns reading their narratives and showing their drawings. Land (5 min): Synthesize Learning (what kinds of myths and stories students want to learn more about; students can revisit their Notice and Wonder Charts). Rigor comes from choosing a visual that adds meaning to the text instead of repeating it.

5. Success Criteria / Evidence of learning

- Students will create a drawing that adds details about a story element or builds excitement or suspense.
- Students will read their narrative fluently and explain how the drawing enhances it.

6. Next steps

Lesson 40, the closing Bookend, shares and celebrates the module. Students create a visual display again in Module 4.

LESSON 40 — Closing Bookend (pp. 406-409)

1. PA Core Standards

None assigned. Great Minds' PA alignment guide does not code Lesson 40 to any standard. Like Lesson 1, it is a reflection and engagement lesson.

2. Learning Goals / I can statements

- I can share knowledge I gained from the module about myths and stories.
- I can reflect on the module topic.

Why it matters: The module closes the way it opened, with a teacher's-choice experience. Students reflect on what they learned about myths, about how stories are told, and about what makes them endure.

3. Student considerations

As in Lesson 1, the Teach Book asks you to customize the lesson to your community and to honor students' home lives, languages and experiences. Option 1 (presenting narratives to a school or community audience) is a powerful finish but stressful for some students; let them choose to read live, play their recording, or share with a smaller audience such as another class or library story time. Option 2 (library research on the history of filmmaking) needs library time arranged in advance. Option 3 (reading an enduring myth or story) works for any class. Depending on the option, plan for more than 60 minutes.

4. Learning Progress / Instructional Plan (pp. 407-409)

Launch (2 min): explain that this is the final day of the module and choral-read the Essential Question. Learn (53 min): Share (8 min), Think-Pair-Share on what students learned about myths and stories, how stories are told, and what makes them endure. Engage (45 min), choose one: (1) present the End-of-Module Task narratives and drawings to a school or community audience; (2) research the history of filmmaking in the library and discuss what students learned; (3) read an enduring myth or story from the volume of reading list and discuss its theme and whether it is still relevant. Land (5 min): connect the experience to the Essential Question with the option-specific question, and encourage students to keep exploring how stories are shared and passed down.

5. Success Criteria / Evidence of learning

Informal; there is no learning task or rubric.
- Students will share at least one thing they learned about myths and stories and one thing about how stories are told.
- Students will explain why a story they heard, read or presented has endured or could endure.

6. Next steps

Module 3 begins. Several skills from this module return there (noticing and wondering, examining evidence, figurative language, central ideas, writers' craft and Socratic seminars), so keep your notes on which students need more support with each.
